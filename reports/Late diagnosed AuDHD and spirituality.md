# Research leaves late-diagnosed AuDHD spirituality unmapped

No published study yet looks at spirituality in people who have both autism and ADHD (AuDHD), and none looks at spirituality in adults diagnosed late with either condition. Everything that can be said today about "AuDHD spirituality" has to be pieced together from autism-only research, ADHD-only research, a small set of late-diagnosis identity studies, and community writing. Read that way, the evidence lines up fairly well. Autistic adults are less likely to fit conventional religion but are not less spiritual: many reach the sacred through the senses, imagination, systems and logic, or through relationships with God or unseen beings that feel easier than human ones. Adults with ADHD drift away from organised worship (about half the odds of regular attendance in one large US study), while private practice such as prayer holds up better. Late diagnosis itself follows the shape of a conversion story (a "not guilty verdict," a re-reading of the whole past, a "true self," a new community), even though researchers describe it only in secular terms. Harm clusters in the social and sensory "container" of religion. Practitioners also point to high-control faith that treated neurodivergent traits as sin, but no study has measured this yet. Adapted mindfulness reduces anxiety for autistic adults in trials. For ADHD adults, mindfulness does no better than good education about ADHD, and meditation harms in neurodivergent people have not been studied at all. For Caroline, the practical point is that the field is wide open. The only two AuDHD lived-experience papers rest on the same six late-diagnosed women, and no book, retreat or study is built for neurodivergent women diagnosed in midlife or later. One caution applies throughout: network limits meant most peer-reviewed sources were read only at abstract level and several community sources only through search snippets. The flags below say which.

## Every finding here is autism-only, ADHD-only, or an extrapolation

The single most important fact for this topic is a gap. Systematic PubMed and web searches found **no peer-reviewed empirical study of religion, belief or spirituality in AuDHD people, adult or child**. The only paper that combined both diagnoses with religious coping was about **mothers** of children with autism or ADHD, not the neurodivergent people themselves ([Berenguer et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1080/20473869.2024.2331748)). Autism studies of religion do not record ADHD as a variable, and ADHD studies do not record autism. The nine empirical adult-autism studies in the most recent review are grouped by diagnosis or trait scores, with no AuDHD subgroup ([Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)). Because ADHD is common among autistic adults, some participants in existing autism samples almost certainly had unrecognised ADHD. Their answers are therefore mixed into the "autism" findings, but there is no way to separate them out.

The AuDHD research that does exist is about identity, not spirituality. Emma Craddock's two papers on six women diagnosed with both conditions in adulthood are the first lived-experience studies of AuDHD ([Craddock 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412); [Craddock 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634593251336163)). A UK study of late diagnosis included three people with both diagnoses ([French & Cassidy 2026](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2024.0085)). These studies matter here because they describe the identity upheaval that community writers link to spiritual change, but none of them asks about faith or meaning in a spiritual sense.

To keep the evidence honest, this report labels findings in plain words. "Autism only" and "ADHD only" mean diagnosed or self-identified samples of one condition. "Trait study" means people from the general population who filled in an autism-trait questionnaire such as the Autism Spectrum Quotient (AQ) but were not diagnosed. "Inference" means a connection drawn by the researchers who prepared these notes or by this report, not one tested in a study.

Network restrictions blocked full-text access to most journal sites during the research, so the depth of checking varies. The table shows what each flag in this report means.

## Autism research: less conventional, not less spiritual

The popular claim that "autistic people are less religious" traces back mainly to one idea and two early studies. The idea comes from the cognitive science of religion and depends on **mentalizing**, the everyday ability to imagine what is going on in someone else's mind (also called "theory of mind"). The argument runs that believing in a personal God means relating to an invisible mind, so people who mentalize less should believe less. A 2012 *PLoS ONE* paper tested this and reported that autistic adolescents believed in God less than controls. In general-population adult samples, higher autism-trait scores predicted weaker belief, with mentalizing (measured indirectly through a self-report empathy questionnaire) explaining the link ([Norenzayan, Gervais & Trzesniewski 2012](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0036880)). The review that examined this paper closely, however, points out that its only diagnosed sample was **11 autistic and 13 neurotypical Florida teenagers, mostly male** ([Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692), read in full). The other early source is a 2011 conference paper, not a journal article. It analysed religion threads on an online autism forum and surveyed 61 adults with "high-functioning autism," finding more atheism, more agnosticism and more **self-built personal belief systems** ([Caldwell-Harris et al. 2011](https://cloudfront.escholarship.org/dist/prd/content/qt6zh3j3pr/qt6zh3j3pr.pdf)). The often-repeated figures (roughly a quarter atheist, and 16% versus 6% building their own belief system) come from that self-selected forum sample (secondary source, worth checking against the original PDF).

Later and larger work has not held this claim up. The biggest direct test, five studies with more than **67,000 participants** across the Netherlands, Switzerland and the US, found that autism-trait and empathy scores did not predict belief in supernatural beings. What did predict belief was **credibility-enhancing displays**: whether people grew up watching those around them actually live out their faith ([Maij et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0182764), trait study). A Finnish study of 2,789 people found mentalizing had only weak links to religious belief ([Lindeman et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2014.09.008), trait study). Among diagnosed adults, a Singapore study of 19 autistic and 19 neurotypical young adults, controlling for upbringing and IQ, found "very little differences" across seven dimensions of religious thinking and behaviour ([Reddish, Tok & Kundt 2016](https://www.ics.muni.cz/en/research/publications/1316052), autism only). An online survey of about 2,000 people, many of them self-identified, went further the other way: higher autistic traits went with **more** spiritual and paranormal experiences and higher religious practice ([Ekblad & Oviedo 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311900234_Religious_cognition_among_subjects_with_Autism_Spectrum_Disorder_ASD_Defective_or_different), mixed diagnosed and self-identified). In an undergraduate sample, belief in God was **uncorrelated** with autistic traits. Overall spirituality was slightly lower, driven by a "search for meaning" subscale ([Crespi et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0213456), trait study). A 2026 study of 2,091 people found that losing faith was linked to unusual-perception traits (called positive schizotypy), not to autistic traits ([Smith et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1689818), trait study). The 2025 review concludes that the mentalizing explanation "has not been unequivocally supported" and that it remains unclear whether autistic people are more, equally or less religious ([Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692), read in full). The fairest summary of the evidence is that autistic adults are **less likely to affiliate with institutional religion and more likely to build their own belief systems**, not that they are less spiritual.

Where autistic adults are religious, the setting shapes how God feels to them. In outpatients at a Dutch Protestant mental-health institution, more autistic traits went with **more fear of God and fewer positive feelings toward God**, and strict Calvinists reported more fear ([Schaap-Jonker et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22588956), autism only). A follow-up with 78 autistic outpatients found faith remained important to them. People with high autistic traits who rated religion as very important more often saw God as a "judge" (described in [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692); the original paper was not retrieved). A second Dutch clinic study did **not** find a more negative image of God in autism and traced negative God images to personality traits instead (same review). Taken together, these studies point to strict theology and personality, rather than autism itself, as the drivers of an anxious relationship with God.

The qualitative research, built on in-depth interviews with small groups, is where autistic spirituality comes most alive. Ingela Visuri's interviews with young Swedish autistic adults suggest that relating to **"bodiless agents"** (God, angels, spirits) can be **less demanding than relating to people**, because there is no face, tone or body language to decode, and that imagined worlds are valued for their predictability and kindness ([Visuri 2018](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1576258106), autism only). Her study of 17 religious or spiritual autistic young adults found they described **many more sensory supernatural experiences**, such as sensed presences, touch or visions with no physical source, than a matched non-autistic group. One summary reports that 14 of the 17 had at least one such experience ([Visuri 2020](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1741048427/Details), autism only; the 14 of 17 figure is from a summary, not the full text). Thirteen UK autistic churchgoers interviewed by van Ommen and Endress found comfort in predictable services and open communities. Loud music, unexpected stimuli, critical sermons and logical inconsistencies in doctrine caused distress, and they described their autistic traits as **both barriers and doorways** to connection with God ([van Ommen & Endress 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00393207221111573), autism only). Research with people with severe nonverbal autism found "a sophisticated and deeply considered spirituality" ([Andersen 2019](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23312521.2019.1682104), autism only).

Two studies connect spirituality to autistic wellbeing. A longitudinal study of **306 autistic adults** found that feeling connected to a higher being predicted greater **psychological flexibility** (the ability to stay open to hard thoughts and feelings while still acting on what matters) and higher life satisfaction 10 weeks later, with flexibility carrying the effect ([Kelley & Aller 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10943-025-02448-7), autism only). A survey of 201 autistic adults, published as a book chapter rather than a journal article, found they have spiritual needs tied to quality of life. Those needs were less pronounced than in other groups, and **secular practices such as mindfulness and meditation** mattered more than religious ones ([Springer chapter 2021](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-70139-0_20), autism only). No trial has tested a spiritual or religious intervention for autistic, ADHD or AuDHD adults. Most "spirituality as coping" research in autism is about parents.

## ADHD research: leaving the pew more than the prayer

The ADHD literature is smaller, almost entirely quantitative, and built mostly from existing US and Israeli datasets. Its clearest finding concerns attendance. In the US Add Health study (N = 9,785), a childhood ADHD diagnosis was "strongly and persistently associated" with **lower religious service attendance in early midlife**, even after adjusting for how religious people were as teenagers. Links with private religiosity such as prayer were largely explained by background factors ([Upenieks & Fishman, JSSR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jssr.70089), ADHD only). A news summary reports this as **about 50% lower odds** of regular attendance, and says the gap was larger for Black and Asian American adults. The race result could not be confirmed from the abstract ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/childhood-adhd-diagnosis-linked-to-lower-adult-religious-attendance/), secondary source). Listings disagree on whether the paper is dated 2025 or 2026. An earlier analysis of more than 8,000 Add Health participants aged 12 to 34 found ADHD symptoms went with lower levels of every religious measure at nearly every wave. In adulthood, prayer and attendance actually **interacted with ADHD to predict worse mental-health outcomes** ([Dew, Kollins & Koenig 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054720972803), ADHD only). An older pilot of 117 adolescent outpatients found no relationship at all ([Dew, Daniel & Koenig 2007](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/702030)).

Other studies point the opposite way, toward religion as a buffer. Among 806 Israeli Jewish adults, religiosity went with fewer ADHD symptoms and softened ADHD's links to distress and antisocial behaviour. Religious **observance** did the buffering, while religious **belonging** went with **more negative attitudes toward ADHD**, a stigma signal ([Novis-Deutsch et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/00207640211005501), ADHD only). Among 578 adults in treatment for substance use who also had ADHD, higher religiousness went with lower ADHD severity, and better self-control did not explain the link ([Begeman et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/00207640251331973), ADHD only). All of this is correlational, so nobody knows whether practice reduces symptoms, people with milder symptoms find it easier to keep practising, or communities provide the support. The World Health Organization's revised functioning framework for ADHD now formally includes **"religion and spirituality"** as a life domain after stakeholder input ([Bölte et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/dmcn.15865)).

The closest hint of an ADHD "spiritual seeker" pattern comes from personality research. Cloninger's Temperament and Character Inventory measures **self-transcendence**, a character trait covering spiritual acceptance and a sense of being part of something larger than oneself. In controlled comparisons (119 adults with ADHD versus 403 controls, and 206 versus 123), adults with ADHD scored **higher on self-transcendence** and on novelty seeking ([Personality profiles in adult ADHD](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4908674/); [J Atten Disord listing](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.1177/1087054715613440), ADHD only). Another study reported adults with ADHD were less mindful but more self-transcendent ([PMC2827240](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2827240), ADHD only; the attribution to Smalley et al. 2009 is prior knowledge). No study measures actual spiritual seeking, conversion or "tradition-hopping" in ADHD. **Hyperfocus**, the intense, hard-to-break absorption many ADHD adults describe, is reported more often by people with ADHD. It correlates only modestly with **flow** (the satisfying "in the zone" state of skilled, effortless focus), and it has costs such as lost time and neglected needs ([Frontiers in Psychiatry 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.886692/full), ADHD only). No peer-reviewed qualitative study of how adults with ADHD experience or define spirituality was found.

Putting the two literatures side by side gives a testable picture of AuDHD spirituality, clearly labelled as inference. From the autism side come sensory, systematic and solitary routes to the sacred and a strong need for predictability. From the ADHD side come friction with scheduled, sit-still services, preserved private practice, and higher self-transcendence and novelty seeking. Someone with both might love the reliable shape of liturgy and still need variety, movement and interest-driven depth. That pull between sameness and novelty mirrors the "two separate parts of my brain" tension that Craddock's AuDHD participants describe in their identities ([Craddock 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634593251336163)). No study has tested this.

## Late diagnosis follows the shape of a conversion story

The late-diagnosis research is the richest body of evidence near this topic, and it consistently describes diagnosis as a turning point in meaning. Most studies use **interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA)**, an in-depth interview method for understanding how individuals make sense of a major experience. Eleven women diagnosed autistic after 40 described a pre-diagnosis life of "pretending to be normal," then "**re-living life through a new lens**." Diagnosis moved them "from being self-critical to self-compassionate," with more agency, although the change was "painful to adjust to at such a late stage" ([Leedham et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319853442), autism only). The one study centred on people **over 50**, the group closest to Caroline's experience, found nine recently diagnosed adults who had "felt isolated and alien" growing up and saw diagnosis as "a **reconfiguration of self**" ([Stagg & Belcher 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/21642850.2019.1684920), autism only). An earlier study titled its findings "**the not guilty verdict**," framing diagnosis as release from a lifetime of implied blame ([Punshon, Skirrow & Murphy 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361309103795), autism only). Oral histories from 26 Australians born before 1975 and diagnosed after 35 describe trauma that shaped their self-image, and diagnosis as a chance to understand their past and feel good about being autistic ([Lilley et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613211050694), autism only). One study took its title from a participant: diagnosis was "the single most important thing that has happened to me in my life" ([Arnold et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2019.0059), autism only). For women, identity and wellbeing changed in a **non-linear** way across the diagnostic journey ([Harmens, Sedgewick & Hobson 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/17455057221137477)), and the central theme was a "quest for acceptance," from oneself and from others ([Harmens et al., Autism in Adulthood 2022](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2021.0016)).

ADHD late diagnosis adds an explicit grief stage. The foundational study of eight UK adults (Young et al. 2007, often cited as 2008) proposed six stages: relief and elation, confusion and turmoil, anger, **sadness and grief**, anxiety, and finally accommodation and acceptance ([Young et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054707305172), ADHD only). Later studies of women emphasise validation more than staged grief. Fourteen women diagnosed in adulthood named "**validation and self-compassion**" as the main benefit ([Babinski & Libsack 2025](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547241297897), ADHD only). Twelve women diagnosed between **45 and 58** called diagnosis "profoundly validating" and described hormonal changes worsening symptoms and medication effects ([Kelly et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547261469625), ADHD only).

AuDHD adds a problem of internal plurality. In Craddock's six late-diagnosed women, life before diagnosis was "**a confusing and traumatising experience**," and **(peri)menopause was often the catalyst for seeking diagnosis "after decades of trauma."** Not knowing their own neurotype was a form of **epistemic injustice**, meaning being wronged in your capacity to know and be believed about your own life. Diagnosis helped them overcome it and moved them toward a feminist standpoint, a politically aware sense of purpose ([Craddock 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412), both conditions). The second paper calls AuDHD a **"residual" category**, a leftover box that diagnostic manuals do not formally name. Living in that box produced ambivalence and fragmented identities, captured in participant phrases such as "**two separate parts of my brain**," "two sides of the same coin," and "autism is a part of me, ADHD is an add-on" ([Craddock 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634593251336163), both conditions). The UK late-diagnosis study, which included three dual-diagnosed people, adds the "burden of a label" and the importance of being ready at the right time ([French & Cassidy 2026](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2024.0085)). A 2026 systematic review of 25 sources, titled with the participant phrase "You become yourself, your full self, the true self," reportedly found that diagnosis moves people toward a **more integrated identity** ([Meldrum et al. 2026](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/25739581261427260), snippet only; the split between autism and ADHD studies is reported inconsistently).

Midlife often brings people to diagnosis through crisis. Autistic women described menopause making it "**impossible to continue to mask**" ([Moseley, Druce & Turner-Cobb 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319901184), autism only). **Masking** means hiding or compensating for neurodivergent traits to fit in. A co-produced study of 24 autistic people called midlife "**a perfect storm**" in which "life experience and adversity converge" ([Brady et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613241244548), autism only). **Autistic burnout**, defined by autistic adults as "chronic exhaustion, loss of skills, and reduced tolerance to stimulus," eases with acceptance, reduced expectations and "**doing things in an autistic way/unmasking**" ([Raymaker et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2019.0079), autism only). Unmasking is therefore both identity work and recovery. An Irish study of women with ADHD found that perimenopause "could bring new compassion and understanding for ADHD" ([Kini-Seery et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/17455057261450178), ADHD only).

Laid out in sequence, these accounts follow the arc of a religious conversion narrative: an alien or false life, a revelation that re-reads the entire past, a true self, absolution, and a new community of "my people." Researchers, however, describe all of it in secular words such as identity, acceptance, self-compassion and epistemic justice. No abstract reviewed used "conversion," "rebirth" or "revelation," and **no study asks late-diagnosed adults about spiritual change**. The only explicitly spiritual framing in this literature comes from an Indigenous study with five Potawatomi participants, in which autism appeared as a "**spiritual gift with purpose**." The authors argue Western frameworks "may miss relational, cultural, and spiritual meanings" ([Kitchens & Wake 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261470856), autism only, not about late diagnosis). **Post-traumatic growth (PTG)**, the psychological idea that people can change for the better after a major struggle, has five standard domains: personal strength, relating to others, new possibilities, appreciation of life, and spiritual or existential change. The late-diagnosis findings map neatly onto the first four. **Spiritual change, the fifth, is the one nobody has studied** (inference). No study has measured PTG in late-diagnosed adults. Popular and professional pieces argue for the link, but they were seen only through search summaries ([Psychology Today 2024](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-neurodivergent-therapist/202406/post-traumatic-growth-after-a-late-autism-diagnosis); [BPS *The Psychologist*](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/you-have-diagnosis-now-what), snippet only).

## Faith communities hurt through the container more than the creed

Congregational research on autistic adults keeps finding that the difficulty lies with the social and sensory setting of religion, not with belief. A quantitative study found autistic churchgoers reported significantly more sensory barriers and needed more accommodations. They also "**felt more connected to God when in control of sensory and social aspects of church**" ([Unwin & van Ommen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/00916471241266810), autism only; the journal is probably the *Journal of Psychology and Theology*, but one summary named a different journal). A church resource page citing that study reports 143 autistic participants, **35% finding church hard because of sensory input**, and "almost a fifth" staying home because of it ([Autism Church](https://www.autismchurch.com/resources), secondary source). An autistic researcher's account of their own experience describes church leaders dismissing concerns about noise, which forced a choice between sensory pain and sitting apart. Belonging came only "when needs were met and autistic embodiment was recognized and valued" ([Waldock 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/23312521.2023.2249452), autism only). Theologian Armand Léon van Ommen names "**normalcy**" as the root cause of autistic people's absence from worship, noting they are overlooked, sometimes infantilised, and given fewer roles ([van Ommen 2023](https://www.baylorpress.com/9781481319911/autism-and-worship/)). Reviewers quote Grant Macaskill: "The church cannot be assumed to constitute a safe and redemptive space for those with autism" ([Christian Century review](https://www.christiancentury.org/review/books/thinking-better-about-autism)). Many autistic adults grew up with this pattern: autistic children are reportedly **1.84 times more likely never to attend worship** ([Whitehead 2018](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jssr.12521); figure from [Key Ministry](https://keyministry.org/2018/07/22/2018-7-22-its-the-hidden-disabilities-that-keep-kids-out-of-church/), secondary source). For ADHD, no qualitative study of adults' own congregational experiences was found, and the familiar labels of "lazy," "rebellious" or "spiritually deficient" appear only in practitioner writing.

Religious trauma is the area where community conviction runs furthest ahead of data. **Religious Trauma Syndrome**, a term coined by psychologist Marlene Winell in 2011, describes symptoms after leaving authoritarian or controlling religion. It is a clinical concept, not a diagnosis in the DSM ([Winell, *CBT Today*](https://journeyfree.org/wp-content/uploads/RTS-article-in-CBT-Today.pdf)). One US survey of 1,581 adults, published in a niche journal, estimated that **27% to 33% have experienced religious trauma in their lifetime** and 10% to 15% are currently affected, with no breakdown by neurotype ([Slade et al. 2023](https://ia801603.us.archive.org/3/items/sherm.2023.vol5.no1.01/Percentage%20of%20U.S.%20Adults%20Suffering%20from%20Religious%20Trauma.pdf)). Autistic adults report higher trauma exposure in general ([PMC12853244](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12853244/)). Clinicians propose specific mechanisms. Autistic literalism and preference for clear categories may take in doctrine and threat messages completely. An autistic mind "often cannot let contradictions go," so hypocrisy hits hard ([Resilient Mind Counseling](https://resilientmindcounseling.com/religious-trauma-neurodivergence-autism-adhd/), practitioner). Among ADHD traits, impulsivity can be moralised as sin, and **rejection sensitivity** (an intense, painful reaction to perceived criticism or rejection, a popular term rather than a formal diagnosis) may amplify shame. **Scrupulosity**, a form of OCD centred on religious or moral fear, is well established in its own right, but no study links it to autism or ADHD in this literature. **No empirical study measures religious trauma, spiritual abuse or scrupulosity in autistic, ADHD or AuDHD adults.** These mechanisms are hypotheses from clinical observation and should be presented that way.

The darkest framings are documented. Deliverance practitioners claim to treat ADHD as the result of the person's or an ancestor's sin ([Mercer 2013](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254320234_Deliverance_demonic_possession_and_mental_illness_some_considerations_for_mental_health_professionals)). Research on parents describes many praying, unsuccessfully, for their autistic children to be delivered from demons ([Journal of Disability & Religion 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23312521.2021.1881024)). Ministry websites still offer "deliverance prayer from autism" ([example](https://authorbeckydvorak.com/deliverance-prayer-from-austism/)). Purity culture's messages about suppressing self and sexuality appear in first-person writing on autism, not surveys ([Canadian Journal of Theology, Mental Health and Disability 2022](https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/cjtmhd/article/view/39544)). No study of adults who survived these experiences was found.

Late diagnosis and **deconstruction** (re-examining and questioning the faith one was raised in, which can end in leaving, staying or rebuilding) appear intertwined in community writing. A counsellor writes that for late-diagnosed autistic adults, deconstruction "isn't just about theology. It is about identity," and that sensory overwhelm and meltdowns were often treated as lapses in faith ([Michelle F. Moseley Counseling](https://michellefmoseley.com/guest-post-religious-trauma-in-late-diagnosed-autistic-adults/), practitioner). Writers argue that masking became a form of discipleship, with sitting still and not questioning praised as virtue, and that people believed they were "bad Christians" for zoning out in sermons ([The Postchurch Files](https://thepostchurchfiles.substack.com/p/why-so-many-neurodivergent-folks), snippet only). An autistic ex-evangelical argues that charismatic faith draws unidentified autistic people because it offers an explanation for their suffering and the hope of being "set free" ([Cat Salladin](https://catsalladin.substack.com/p/charismatic-evangelicalism-and-neurodivergence), snippet only). The ADHD attendance data support a distinction that community writing often blurs: **leaving church is well evidenced, while leaving faith is not**. Some of the lower attendance probably reflects exclusion and access barriers rather than choice (inference).

What helps is consistent across studies and can be summarised as **predictability plus agency**. Autistic interviewees described liturgy as "predictable and easy to learn and memorize" ([van Ommen & Endress 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/00393207221111573)). Feeling in control of sensory and social input brought people closer to God ([Unwin & van Ommen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/00916471241266810)). Real belonging followed when needs were actually met ([Waldock 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/23312521.2023.2249452)). A Lilly-funded initiative argues for flexible, "openhanded" worship ([Christianity Today 2025](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2025/07/autism-worship-church-neurodivergence-inclusion-research/)). That sounds like the opposite of predictability, but the likely resolution is a predictable structure combined with freedom in how each body takes part. Journalism repeatedly notes that sensory rooms and headphones are necessary but not sufficient ([RNS via U.S. News 2025](https://www.usnews.com/news/best-states/new-jersey/articles/2025-07-22/in-temples-and-churches-sensory-rooms-remove-barriers-for-neurodivergent-worshipers)). Affirming resources are concentrated in UK academic theology, especially Aberdeen's [Centre for Autism and Theology](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/dhpa/research/centres/centre-for-autism-and-theology/resources/), and in autistic-authored theology such as Claire Williams's *Peculiar Discipleship*, which calls for "radical hospitality" of attention, access, acceptance and imagination ([SCM Press](https://scmpress.hymnsam.co.uk/books/9780334063063/peculiar-discipleship)). Jewish and Muslim research and resources focus almost entirely on families and children ([Sher, Gibson & Sher 2022](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13623613221075099)). An autistic Muslim woman writes that without neuro-affirming resources, neurodivergent Muslim women are shut out of the knowledge and community they need ([Emna Malas, ABC Religion & Ethics](https://www.abc.net.au/religion/islamic-worship-from-an-autistic-muslimah-perspective/106442562)). No study compares wellbeing in affirming and non-affirming congregations.

## Sensory intensity opens doors that standard meditation can close

Several theories suggest autistic perception could favour mystical or transcendent experience, but none has been tested against spiritual outcomes. **Monotropism**, developed by autistic thinkers Dinah Murray and Wenn Lawson with Mike Lesser, proposes that autistic attention is pulled deeply into a few interests at a time rather than spread across many ([monotropism.org](https://monotropism.org/?p=62); original 2005 paper, doi 10.1177/1362361305051398, prior knowledge). A 2024 paper builds an "autistic flow theory" on it ([Heasman et al. 2024](https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/towards-autistic-flow-theory-a-non-pathologising-conceptual-appro/)). A practitioner quoted in the British Psychological Society's magazine says autistic people in monotropic flow "truly are in a meditative state," with a claimed brainwave shift that is not backed by cited data ([BPS *The Psychologist*](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/autism-therapy-monotropism-meditation-and-autistic-flow), practitioner). No source found has the monotropism theorists themselves framing it as a theory of spiritual experience. **Intense World Theory** proposes that autistic brains over-perceive, over-attend and over-feel, with withdrawal as self-protection, but its core evidence comes largely from animal models ([Favre, Markram & Markram 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/17588928.2019.1592143)). **Absorption**, a personality trait describing how fully someone can be taken over by an experience, image or idea, predicts mystical and awe experiences in the general population ([Lifshitz, van Elk & Luhrmann 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053810018305257)). Nobody has measured absorption in diagnosed autistic or ADHD adults alongside spiritual experience. Single-pointed monotropic focus resembles concentration meditation, which makes this a promising bridge, but it is still theory (inference).

The direct evidence on transcendent experience is small but consistent. A 2026 study of 93 autistic and 99 non-autistic adults found autistic adults showed **no reduction in awe**, but differed in time perception, the physical side of awe, and the sources they named ([Srinivasan, Yaden & Keltner 2026](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/402528124_The_Architecture_of_Awe_Experience_Disposition_and_Meaning-Making_in_Autism), preprint, abstract and summaries only; autism only). Its lead author is autistic, and its co-authors include a leading awe researcher (author backgrounds are prior knowledge). Visuri's sensed-presence findings point the same way. Self-selected surveys of autistic psychedelic users report mystical features such as unity and transcendence. In one survey of 233 users, **82% credited their most impactful experience with reduced distress**, while **about 20% reported undesirable effects**, some among the worst experiences of their lives ([Stroud et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-024-06685-8), autism only). The authors caution that self-selected, retrospective data cannot show cause.

Some popular claims do not survive contact with the evidence. **Synaesthesia**, where one sense triggers another (such as seeing colours with sounds), is reportedly more common in autism (about 19% versus 7% in one study: [Baron-Cohen et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1186/2040-2392-4-40), prior knowledge, figures not re-checked). Another study found the higher rate only in autistic people with savant skills ([Hughes et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1163/22134808-00002558)). People with mirror-touch synaesthesia, who feel touch when they see someone else touched, did **not** show enhanced empathy, even though 30% of them reported an autism diagnosis ([Baron-Cohen et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0160543)). Autistic adults made **fewer** conventional cross-sense associations than others, which argues against a general "enhanced intuition" ([Król et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261441750)). **Interoception**, the sense of what is happening inside your own body (heartbeat, breath, hunger), shows a revealing mismatch in autism: lower accuracy at detecting heartbeats alongside **inflated** self-reported body sensitivity, with the gap linked to anxiety ([Garfinkel et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2015.12.003)). No evidence supports "empath," psychic or "sensing energy" claims in neurodivergent spiritual communities. Sensed presences and intense sensory moments can still be honoured as real experiences without making claims about where they come from.

Contemplative practice helps when it is adapted. In the first trial for autistic adults, 42 people were randomly assigned to a nine-week mindfulness programme that removed metaphors and used concrete instructions, or to a waiting list. The programme reduced depression, anxiety and rumination ([Spek, van Ham & Nyklíček 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22964266/), autism only). A 2025 network meta-analysis (a method that compares many treatments at once across trials) pooled 67 trials with 3,604 autistic participants. **Mindfulness-based programmes ranked best for anxiety**, with a large standardised effect of about 0.84, though samples were mostly male ([Ding et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1660412), autism only). For ADHD adults the picture is weaker. Against inactive comparisons such as waiting lists, mindfulness shows medium to large benefits. Against **active comparisons**, where the control group also gets a real intervention, benefits shrink to low or negligible ([Oliva et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2021.05.068), ADHD only). In the first head-to-head trial, 81 unmedicated adults improved equally with mindfulness or structured education about ADHD ([Hoxhaj et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-018-0868-4), ADHD only). No mindfulness trial has been run specifically in AuDHD adults.

Meditation can also harm, and neurodivergent people may be at higher risk, though this has not been studied directly. Willoughby Britton's lab documented more than 50 kinds of challenging meditation experiences, spanning body sensations, emotion, thinking, perception, motivation, sense of self and social life ([Lindahl et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5443484)). A systematic review reported adverse effects in roughly 8% of meditators ([Farias et al. 2020, via The Swaddle](https://www.theswaddle.com/why-meditation-works-for-some-people-but-not-others), prior knowledge for the figure, secondary source). No peer-reviewed study measures meditation harms in autistic or ADHD adults. Clinicians at a Toronto adult neurodevelopmental centre report that body scans, breath focus and loving-kindness practice can trigger adverse sensory responses, and that many neurodivergent people call themselves mindfulness "drop-outs" ([Mindful.org](https://www.mindful.org/learning-to-celebrate-neurodiversity-in-mindfulness/), practitioner). ADHD carries a higher risk of PTSD ([Magdi et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-025-02774-7)), trauma history shapes difficult meditation experiences, and autistic interoception is mismatched. Together these suggest late-diagnosed AuDHD adults face elevated risk on intensive silent retreats (inference). The best-tested body-awareness approach for autistic adults gives feedback rather than open-ended inward attention: six sessions of heartbeat-detection training cut anxiety in 121 autistic adults, with **31% meeting recovery criteria versus 16% of controls** ([Quadt et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2021.101042), autism only). Stimming and special interests have documented positive regulating and connecting functions ([Bates et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/25739581261424784)). Framing stimming as prayer or special interests as devotion is a community interpretation, not a tested finding. Community practice already reflects these lessons. Autsit, an autistic meditation group led by Soto Zen teacher Anlor Davin, runs one-hour sessions of 30 minutes sitting plus light discussion, because standard retreats are too crowded and demanding ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/anlor-davin/)). A dedicated neurodivergent meditation retreat ran in North Carolina in July 2026, capped at 50 people ([Neurospicy Retreats](https://neurospicyretreats.com/), snippet only).

## Books and voices fill the space research left empty

The book landscape is mostly Christian and mostly about autism. Olga Bogdashina's *Autism and Spirituality* (2013) is still the only book-length, tradition-neutral treatment of autistic inner and spiritual life, and it predates both the neurodiversity-affirming movement and the late-diagnosis wave ([JKP](https://us.jkp.com/products/autism-and-spirituality)). Late diagnosis appears in these books as biography rather than as a subject. Poet and professor Daniel Bowman Jr., **diagnosed at 35**, wrote *On the Spectrum: Autism, Faith, and the Gifts of Neurodiversity* (2021), the most visible late-diagnosed autistic Christian memoir ([Christianity Today](https://www.christianitytoday.com/ct/2021/september/daniel-bowman-spectrum-understanding-autism-from-inside.html)). Pastor Lamar Hardwick, **diagnosed at 36**, wrote *How Ableism Fuels Racism* (2024), which makes him nearly alone in bringing race into neurodivergent theology ([Baker Publishing](https://bakerpublishinggroup.com/books/how-ableism-fuels-racism/415830)). Church of England vicar John Allister's *Faith, Hope and Autism* includes letters to his undiagnosed younger self ([Neurodiverse Church](https://www.neurodiversechurch.org/faith-hope-and-autism-learning-to-love-neurodiversity/)). Other key titles are Macaskill's *Autism and the Church* (2019), van Ommen's *Autism and Worship* (2023), Williams's *Peculiar Discipleship* (2023), Amy Kenny's disability-justice book *My Body Is Not a Prayer Request* (2022), and UCC pastor Sarah Griffith Lund's *Blessed Minds* (2025) ([RNS](https://religionnews.com/2025/02/20/sarah-lund-provokes-readers-to-see-the-neurodiversity-in-god-and-scripture/)). A 2024 to 2025 wave of ADHD-and-faith books is almost entirely self-published Christian devotional or how-to material. Richard Lowe's *The ADHD Christian* is the **only book found that names AuDHD at all**, and only as a sub-topic ([Google Books](https://books.google.de/books/about/The_ADHD_Christian.html?id=_rez0QEACAAJ&redir_esc=y)). Outside Christianity there is one Buddhist anthology, *Autism and Buddhist Practice* ([JKP](https://us.jkp.com/products/autism-and-buddhist-practice)), one family-centred Islamic book ([Warsi & Memon](https://thirdplacebooks.com/book/9781975509712)), and a small neurodivergent witchcraft niche led by Jenny C. Bell's *Divergent Witchcraft* ([Agenda Bookshop](https://agendabookshop.com/products/divergent-witchcraft)). General AuDHD books for late-discovered women, such as Sarah Hendrickx's *The Complete Guide to AuDHD*, do not centre spirituality ([Hachette UK](https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/sarah-hendrickx/the-complete-guide-to-audhd/9781839979972/)).

The most visible late-diagnosed voices write newsletters and host podcasts, and most come from or after US and UK evangelicalism (newsletter pages could not be opened, so contents come from search metadata and snippets). D.L. Mayfield, assessed for autism in her late 30s, renamed her newsletter from "God Is My Special Interest" to "**Healing is My Special Interest**," about "late-diagnosed neurodivergence and repairing from high control religion" ([Mayfield](https://dlmayfield.substack.com/p/unholy-mess-where-god-and-neurodivergence)). On the SquarePeg podcast she discussed deconstructing religious identity as a newly identified autistic woman and claimed that Christian groups contain many undiagnosed autistic women ([SquarePeg #82](https://squarepeg.community/82-s7-ep2-god-as-a-special-interest-deconstructing-religious-identity-as-a-newly-identified-autistic/)). The explicitly AuDHD spiritual voices are mostly solo practitioners. One UK spiritual director, **AuDHD and diagnosed in her 40s**, writes that long, heavily verbal services are hard for neurodivergent minds ([Spiritual Direction UK](https://spiritualdirectionuk.substack.com/p/neurodivergent-spirituality-spiritual), snippet only; author's name not captured). Linda Carlson describes a late autism diagnosis plus ADHD, with **menopause stripping away masking** and prompting spiritual deconstruction and reframing ([Caring Hands Refuge](https://caringhandsrefuge.substack.com/p/middle-age-neurodiversity-and-spirituality), snippet only). Bridgette Hamstead's "The AuDHD Guide to Spirituality" is a newsletter piece, not a book ([Substack](https://bridgettehamstead.substack.com/p/the-audhd-guide-to-spirituality), partly verified). Neuropsychologist Dr. Rabia Subhani, a late-diagnosed ADHDer and Sufi, hosts *The NeuroDivine: Spirituality & Neurodivergence* ([Apple Podcasts](https://podcasts.apple.com/podcast/id1632736304)), and Stephanie Elizabeth's *AuDHD Magic* covers autistic and ADHD mysticism ([Buzzsprout](https://audhdmagic.buzzsprout.com/1731599/episodes/17909149-wtf-is-audhd-magic-the-origin-story)). A Welsh spiritual mentor, Dr. Kate Tomas, is reported to be diagnosed autistic and ADHD in her late 30s and to run workshops for late-diagnosed autistic women, but sources conflicted on her details ([SquarePeg #81](https://squarepeg.community/81-s7-ep1-the-challenge-of-creating-the-life-you-want-when-you-diverge-from-the-neurotypical-norm/), verify). Among clergy, Catholic priest Fr. Matthew Schneider was diagnosed shortly after ordination ([Patheos](https://www.patheos.com/blogs/throughcatholiclenses/2020/11/adult-autism-diagnosis-changes-ministry/)). A Church of England priest felt safe disclosing his autism only after **eight years in ministry** ([Church Times 2023](https://www.churchtimes.co.uk/articles/2023/19-may/features/features/autism-adventures-beyond-the-neurotypical)), even though the Church of England estimates that "perhaps 1 in every 7" of its clergy and leaders are neurodivergent ([Church of England](https://www.churchofengland.org/resources/barrier-free-belonging/neurodiversity)).

Six themes recur in this community writing: masking learned in church and religious trauma tied to unrecognised traits; God or faith as a special interest, including intellectual, systematic faith; questioning and justice sensitivity treated as spiritual integrity rather than rebellion ([Lovette Jallow](https://lovettejallow.substack.com/p/why-autistic-kids-question-religion), snippet only); sensory and verbal overload in worship set against the comfort of liturgy and repetition; grief, deconstruction and reframing after diagnosis, often around midlife or menopause; and contemplative practice adapted for neurodivergent nervous systems. "God as pattern," nature and animals as neurodivergent spirituality, and Reddit discussion did not surface in searches, so they are gaps in this research, not evidence of absence. Organised provision is thin. Institutional work (Church of England, Cambridge's Faraday Institute, the Aberdeen centre) is mostly about making churches inclusive, not about neurodivergent adults' own spiritual formation or recovery. Training for spiritual directors on neurodivergence is only beginning, with a free online workshop in September 2026 ([Canadian Jubilee](https://www.canadianjubilee.ca/events/wired-for-sacred-space-supporting-neurodivergence-in-spiritual-direction)). A K.T. Sancken post notes that most autism-and-faith books are aimed at children ([Substack](https://ktsancken.substack.com/p/3-resources-for-late-diagnosed-autistic), snippet only). **No retreat, cohort or programme was found for AuDHD adults, late-diagnosed adults, or neurodivergent women over 50.** Post-religious and "spiritual but not religious" late-diagnosed adults are served almost entirely by individual newsletters, podcasts and coaches.

## Seven gaps define the white space

The weaknesses of the existing research are well documented. Samples are tiny, mostly male and often adolescent. Many studies use trait questionnaires in place of diagnosis, or self-identified online samples. Mentalizing is framed as a deficit, terminology is inconsistent across studies ([Hayat et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.61271)), and the evidence base is almost entirely Western and Christian, with tools built in Western traditions ([Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692), read in full). The typical research participant is a young, male, Western, Christian-background autistic person. Late-diagnosed adults, women, nonbinary people and older adults, the groups that make up much of the late-diagnosed AuDHD population, are essentially missing (inference from sample descriptions). No meta-analysis of autism and religiosity exists.

| Gap | What exists now | Confidence that the gap is real |
|---|---|---|
| AuDHD and spirituality | No studies; one book mentions AuDHD in passing | Very high |
| Late diagnosis as a spiritual or existential event | Rich memoir and newsletter writing; no qualitative or PTG study | High |
| Religious trauma in neurodivergent adults | Practitioner hypotheses; general-population trauma estimates with no neurotype breakdown | High |
| ADHD adults' lived spiritual experience | Large correlational datasets; no qualitative study | High |
| Women 50+, perimenopause and spirituality | Anecdotes (Carlson); menopause studies that never ask about meaning or faith | High |
| Meditation harms and adapted practice for AuDHD | Autism-only and ADHD-only mindfulness trials; no AuDHD trial; no neurodivergent harm study | High |
| Non-Christian, non-Western, LGBTQ+ and racially diverse voices | One Buddhist anthology, family-focused Jewish and Muslim work, an Indigenous study of five people, Hardwick on race; nothing on LGBTQ+ neurodivergent spirituality | High for LGBTQ+ and non-Western; moderate for others |

Several items in the notes still need checking before anyone quotes them publicly: the full Meldrum 2026 review, the race result in the ADHD attendance study, the Visuri control-group figures, the Unwin and van Ommen journal and percentages, the Caldwell-Harris atheism figures, and the authors behind several Substack posts. The most valuable next research step is a direct search of Reddit communities (r/AutisticWithADHD, r/autisticadults, r/exchristian, r/Deconstruction), Bluesky and YouTube, where much late-diagnosed discussion happens and which this research could not reach.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the usual question. Asking whether neurodivergent people are "more or less religious" produced fifteen years of contradictory results. The better question is which spiritual containers fit which nervous systems. On that question the scattered evidence converges: predictable structure, control over sensory and social input, freedom to build one's own beliefs, private and interest-led practice, short and adapted contemplative formats, and feedback-based rather than open-ended body awareness. Late diagnosis looks like the moment when many adults first get permission to ask which container fits. Its conversion-like structure, combined with the field's complete silence on spiritual change as a growth domain, suggests that late diagnosis is an unrecognised spiritual turning point, especially for women whose diagnosis arrives through menopause and burnout. This is a hypothesis worth testing, not a finding.

For Caroline's training and tools, the evidence marks clear lines between what is safe to claim and what is not. It is safe to say that autistic adults are less conventional rather than less spiritual, that ADHD is linked to lower attendance rather than lost faith, that sensory and social barriers drive exclusion, and that adapted mindfulness helps autistic anxiety. It is not supported to say that autistic people are less religious, that neurodivergent people are empaths or unusually intuitive, that hyperfocus equals contemplative absorption, or that religious trauma is proven to be more common in neurodivergent adults. Anything described as "AuDHD spirituality" should be labelled as built from separate autism and ADHD evidence. The integration task that Craddock's participants describe, holding "two separate parts of my brain" together, may be the most promising design principle available. A practice that gives the autistic side reliable form and the ADHD side movement and novelty would be the first one built for this group, and no one has built it yet.
