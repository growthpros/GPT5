# Give the puzzle away, sell the expertise

The honest answer first: a standalone "type a theme, get a word search" generator is a weak business, and the evidence is consistent on that point. The AI theme step, which feels like the magic, is already free in many places (Template.net, Supercoloring, Canva, The Teacher's Corner, KDPBuilder), and the strongest free incumbents already give away creating, printing, answer keys, online play and sharing. Where people do pay, they pay very little: about $5 to $11 a month for a teacher puzzle tool, about $3 to $5 for a one-off personalised printable, and about $75 a year for a whole senior-activity library that includes word searches. At those prices, a solo founder needs hundreds of paying subscribers and traffic in the hundreds of thousands of visits a month to earn a modest income, competing against sites that have had a ten-year head start. The stronger option is to treat your puzzle as what it already is: a lovely, accessibility-first demonstration piece that brings the right people to Growth Partner Pros and shows, in thirty seconds, what thoughtful AI design for neurodivergent users looks like. If you would also like to test paid demand, the most promising small experiment is a large-print, calm-design version for senior living activity staff and special education teachers, tested with a cheap printable pack before you build accounts or payments. Running costs are tiny (about a hundredth of a cent per puzzle), so the real costs are your time, security, and legal care around children and health claims. The researchers found no direct willingness-to-pay data, no reliable search volumes and no revenue figures for any word search generator, so the demand side of this picture rests on observed list prices and my reasoning, and I flag that throughout.

A quick note on how to read this report. When I say **verified**, the researchers read the figure on the vendor's or official body's own page on 13 November 2026. When I say **reported**, the figure came from a search summary, a third-party tracker or a vendor blog and is worth re-checking before you rely on it. When I say **my inference**, it is reasoning from the evidence, not a sourced fact.

## The AI theme step is already free almost everywhere

Your product's headline feature is typing any theme and letting AI choose the words. Verified from their own pages: Template.net offers a free prompt-to-puzzle generator with no sign-up in 20+ languages ([Template.net](https://www.template.net/ai-word-search-generator)); Supercoloring builds an AI word list from a topic, difficulty and word count, free for personal and school use ([Supercoloring](https://www.supercoloring.com/tool/word-search-generator)); KDPBuilder gives a theme-to-grid tool with answer key away as a lead magnet for its paid book studio ([KDPBuilder](https://kdpbuilder.com/free-tools/word-search-generator)); and The Teacher's Corner offers an "A.I. list" option inside an established tool that already has online play and sharing ([Teacher's Corner](https://worksheets.theteacherscorner.net/make-your-own/word-search/)). Canva also lists a free word search app that works from AI prompts, though that one is reported from search snippets because Canva blocked direct reading ([Canva app](https://www.canva.com/apps/AAG83en-zjY/word-search-maker)).

The big legacy names have not added AI on the pages checked, but they give away almost everything else. Word Search Labs is free with no ads, no watermarks and no registration, and includes shaped grids, printing with or without answers, a shareable link for solving online, and embed code ([Word Search Labs](https://wordsearchlabs.com/)). Super Teacher Worksheets offers a free unlimited generator with a printable PDF and solution page ([Super Teacher Worksheets](https://www.superteacherworksheets.com/generator-word-search.html)). Discovery's Puzzlemaker is fully free ([Puzzlemaker](https://puzzlemaker.discoveryeducation.com/word-search)). So a new product cannot charge for making, printing, answer keys, or basic online play, because those are free a click away.

Here is what the paid competitors charge. All rows are verified from the vendor's page unless noted.

**My inference:** the ceiling for an individual buyer is roughly $5 to $11 a month. The only place a single customer is worth more is publishers and brands buying from Amuse Labs at $99 a month and up, and Amuse Labs already includes AI and word search on every tier, including the free one.

The consumer app route is even harder. The top word search app, Word Search Explorer, is reported to earn only about $15,000 to $22,000 a week in US in-app purchases despite hundreds of thousands of weekly downloads, with advertising supplying most of the money, and it is owned by a studio with its own ad technology ([Sensor Tower](https://sensortower.com/blog/2025-q4-unified-top-5-word-puzzle-games-revenue-us-603697f4241bc16eb8570d37); [MTG](https://www.mtg.com/casual-district/playsimple/)). Acquiring one app install costs about $0.56 on a global average and is rising ([Adjust 2026 summary](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/adjust-gaming-app-insights-report)). A solo founder without an advertising budget cannot realistically play that game. The New York Times' Strands, a themed word-find, was played 1.5 billion times in 2025 ([NBC New York](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/entertainment/entertainment-news/hardest-wordle-connections-games-2025/6436464/?amp=1)), which proves people love themed word-finding. But that love goes to a daily, hand-edited ritual from a famous brand, not to on-demand generation.

Selling the output instead of the tool does not rescue the numbers. Printable word search packs of 75 to 100 puzzles sell for about $2 to $4 on Etsy ([Etsy listing](https://www.etsy.com/listing/1806953412); [Etsy listing](https://www.etsy.com/listing/4452104530)). Puzzle-book publishers on Amazon can already buy AI theme-to-book tools cheaply, with lifetime bundles from about $39 to $357 ([Bearwood Labs](https://bearwoodlabs.gumroad.com/l/puzzle-bundle); [Self Publishing Titans](https://selfpublishingtitans.com/all-puzzle-tools-sales)), and one vendor's own figures put the royalty on a $9.99 large-print book at about $1.74 a copy ([KDP Easy](https://www.kdpeasy.com/blog/large-print-word-search-seniors)). Amazon also requires publishers to disclose AI-generated text ([KDP Content Guidelines](https://kdp.amazon.com/help/topic/G200672390)).

## Every observed price is small, so the sums need big traffic

The researchers looked for revenue figures from any word search generator and found none. That absence is itself worth noticing. What they did find points the same way. BrightSprout says over 250,000 word searches have been created on it since 2013 ([BrightSprout](https://brightsprout.com/word-search-maker)), which is under 20,000 a year. Semrush estimates, reported at snippet level, put WordMint at about 297,000 visits a month and TheWordSearch.com at about 439,000 ([Semrush](https://vi.semrush.com/website/thewordsearch.com/competitors)). A solitaire site with about 895,000 monthly visitors reported only $3,521 in ad revenue ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/simple-as-solitaire-277b2e55ea)).

Freemium products typically convert 3% to 5% of free sign-ups into payers when things go well, and a quarter convert below 2.5% ([ChartMogul](https://chartmogul.com/reports/saas-conversion-report/), reported).

**My inference, as a worked example:** imagine 10,000 visitors a month, 1 in 10 signs up, and 3% of those pay $5 a month. That is 30 payers and about $150 a month. To reach even $3,000 a month you would need roughly 600 payers, which on the same ratios means about 200,000 visitors a month. That is WordMint-sized traffic, which WordMint built over many years with a library of 300,000+ ready-made puzzles. Payment fees bite hardest at these low prices too: on a $5 sale, a tax-handling payment service charging 5% plus $0.50 keeps about $0.75, or 15% ([Dodo Payments](https://dodopayments.com/blogs/paddle-vs-lemon-squeezy/), reported).

Using the puzzle as a marketing give-away to collect email addresses in the usual way is also unproven. The only data point found is one marketer's own test, where a word search lead magnet converted at 9% against 61% for a plain checklist ([Lilach Bullock](https://www.lilachbullock.com/create-printable-word-search-puzzles-for-customers/)). So I am not suggesting the puzzle as a sign-up bribe. I am suggesting it as a demonstration of your expertise, which is a different job, and I explain that in the recommended path.

## Calm, large-print design is the open space, with demand unproven

Here is the encouraging part. None of the competitor pages the researchers read mention large print, dyslexia-friendly fonts, screen-reader support or colour-contrast options. The closest were font and colour choices at Teach Starter and The Teacher's Corner ([Teach Starter](https://www.teachstarter.com/us/widget/word-search-us/); [Teacher's Corner](https://worksheets.theteacherscorner.net/make-your-own/word-search/)). Your product's no-timer, gentle-hint, legible-font design already sits in that space. Please hold this lightly, though: it is an absence on marketing pages, not a tested finding, and nobody checked how each tool actually behaves on a phone.

Looking at the segments in order of promise:

**Senior living and memory care activity staff** look the most promising for paid use. They have budgeted, recurring spend: Golden Carers charges $74.95 a year for an activity library that includes word searches, and members there say they like bigger letters and describe enlarging grids by hand before printing ([Golden Carers](https://goldencarers.com/); [Golden Carers word search page](https://goldencarers.com/word-search-occupations/6206)). Activity Connection advertises its annual plan as "less than 55¢ per day" ([Activity Connection](https://activityconnection.com/?p=823), reported). The weakness is plain: for about $75 a year they get a whole library, so a word-search-only tool would need to cost well under that, or win on speed and personal touches such as residents' names and local places.

**Neurodivergent users and the people who support them** are the best fit with where you are taking your business, and no competitor leads here. The candid caveat is that the researchers found no peer-reviewed evidence that word searches help ADHD or autistic people, no community discussion, and no sign anyone pays a premium. The visible products are $6 to $12 puzzle books ([Lulu](https://www.lulu.com/shop/dk-puzzle-books/adhd-word-search-book/paperback/product-7kvzd6w.html)). So this niche gives you credibility and a way to reach people. It does not yet give you proven demand. One more gentle caution: dense letter grids with backwards and diagonal words are hard for many dyslexic readers, so a "dyslexia friendly" label needs testing with real users first.

**Teachers** are the largest audience and spend real money from their own pockets, an average of $895 a year in the US and A$988 in Australia ([AdoptAClassroom](https://www.adoptaclassroom.org/2025/06/09/2025-teacher-survey-spending-stats-classroom-needs/); [AEU](https://www.aeufederal.org.au/news-media/media-releases/2025/teachers-spending-175-million-their-own-money-keep-classrooms-running-1)). But this is the most crowded segment, an influential teaching blog calls word searches the worst kind of "busysheet" ([Cult of Pedagogy](https://www.cultofpedagogy.com/?p=15374)), and teachers usually already have their word list (this week's vocabulary), so your AI step saves them the least effort.

**Event hosts** do pay, about $4 to $5 for a personalised wedding or baby shower printable ([Etsy](https://www.etsy.com/listing/1332219508/); [Gumroad](https://sophieadams.gumroad.com/l/bridal-word-search)), but they are buying a pretty finished design, once. **Puzzle-book self-publishers** pay the most for tools, yet that market is crowded and a poor match for your brand.

## The features worth building, in order of value

Since free tools cover the basics, the features that matter are the ones that make your version the most comfortable to use, not the most powerful.

**Comfort and accessibility settings (your real difference):** adjustable grid size and letter spacing, a cream or soft pastel background, a large-print mode, and a switch to turn off diagonal and backwards words. These follow the British Dyslexia Association guidance on sans-serif fonts, larger spacing and off-white backgrounds, as described in secondary copies of the guide ([Wigan Council copy](https://www.wigan.gov.uk/Docs/PDF/Business/Professionals/SEN/HEFA/Dyslexia-Style-Guide.pdf)). Keeping the page free of ads, streaks and animation belongs here too.

**Print-ready output:** a one-click printable page with an answer key. Senior living staff and teachers print far more than they play on screen.

**A share link with no login:** so a carer or teacher can send a puzzle to someone else to solve.

**Safety checks:** a block on unsuitable themes, and a scan of the finished grid for accidental rude words formed by the random filler letters. Both Puzzlemaker and Super Teacher Worksheets leave that proofreading to the user ([Puzzlemaker](https://puzzlemaker.discoveryeducation.com/word-search)), so doing it for people is a genuine kindness and a selling point for care homes and schools.

**Only if you decide to charge:** sign-in, saved puzzle collections, and a hosted checkout. This is my inference about a minimum paid version; the researchers found no source confirming what paying users expect.

I would leave full screen-reader support off the promise list for now. It is technically hard for a letter grid and no existing product was found that solves it.

## Running costs are pennies; the real exposure is legal and security

**AI cost (verified from official pricing pages):** the cheapest current models cost $0.05 to $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.40 to $0.50 per million output tokens ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing.md); [Google](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). The researchers' arithmetic turns that into roughly $0.09 to $0.15 per 1,000 puzzles. In plain terms, 10,000 puzzles a month costs about a dollar or two. Models do get retired and repriced, though: one Google model reroutes to a different one on 1 December 2026, and another doubles in price on 1 January 2027, so a little ongoing upkeep is part of the deal.

**Hosting and payments (reported, not read on official pages):** a starter setup runs from $0 to about $45 a month, for example Supabase Pro at $25 and Vercel Pro at $20 ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/pricing.md); [Developers Digest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/tools/cloud-cost-compare)). Plain Stripe is 2.9% plus $0.30 and leaves sales tax to you; a "merchant of record" such as Paddle charges about 5% plus $0.50 and handles tax collection for you ([dev.to comparison](https://dev.to/jettfu/stripe-vs-paddle-vs-lemon-squeezy-fee-comparison-2026-2c77); [Dodo Payments](https://dodopayments.com/blogs/paddle-vs-lemon-squeezy/)). With clients in the UK and Australia, the second kind is likely worth the extra fee, though the UK VAT and Australian GST rules themselves were not researched.

**Security:** this matters most for a non-developer. Scans of apps built with AI app builders repeatedly found exposed API keys, open databases and no rate limiting ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/why-vibe-coded-apps-are-still-leaking-user-data-in-2026-4a6ab2fbdd); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/lovable-vibe-coding-security-crisis-exposed), both reported). Because one puzzle costs a hundredth of a cent, ordinary users will not run up a bill. The risk is a leaked key being used by someone else for expensive work. The protections are a key kept on the server, a hard monthly spending cap in the AI provider's console, and a limit on requests per visitor.

**Children:** the amended US children's privacy rule (COPPA) has been fully in force since 22 April 2026 ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments), reported), and the UK Children's Code applies to services "likely to be accessed by children" ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/introduction-to-the-childrens-code), reported). The safest design, by the researchers' reasoning, is to serve adults (teachers, carers, activity staff) who print or share puzzles, with no child accounts, no advertising and no tracking on shared pages.

**Health claims:** please avoid any wording like "improves focus", "calms" or "protects memory". Lumosity paid $2 million to settle US regulator charges over unproven brain-training claims ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/44800)). The best-known puzzle study is a correlation, not proof ([University of Exeter](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/39482)). "Enjoyable, calm and yours to control" is both honest and safe.

**Copyright and brands:** the US Copyright Office says wholly AI-generated output is not protected by copyright ([Copyright Alliance](https://copyrightalliance.org/ai-report-part-2-copyrightability/), reported), so your word lists are not an asset anyone can own. Your brand, your design and anything you curate by hand are. A visitor privately making a Harry Potter puzzle is low risk for you; the researchers' reading is that takedown trouble arrives when a seller publishes or sells brand-named puzzles ([Marmalead](https://blog.marmalead.com/etsy-copyright-infringement/), reported).

**Accessibility lawsuits:** about 3,117 US website accessibility cases were counted in 2025, with small businesses often the target ([Clym](https://www.clym.io/blog/accessibility-lawsuits-2025-small-business-websites), reported). For a product whose promise is accessibility, getting keyboard use and contrast right is part of the brand as well as the protection. None of this is legal advice, and a short conversation with a lawyer is sensible before you take payments.

## A gentle, staged path that protects your time

Everything below is my recommendation, built from the evidence above. The time estimates are my own rough guesses for a solo, non-developer founder working around client commitments, and I have already doubled them, as you prefer. Nothing was sourced on build times apart from one developer who built a daily word search in about a week ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/i-made-jumblie-a-daily-word-search-game-44fc9ef768)).

**Stage 1: Make the free page safe and unmistakably yours (about 4 to 6 weeks of part-time effort)**

1. You could start by checking that the AI key lives on the server and not in the page itself. Asking whoever or whatever built the page "is my API key visible in the browser?" is enough to begin.
2. Next, it would help to set a hard monthly spending cap in your AI provider's console. Something like $10 is plenty at these prices.
3. Then add the comfort settings one at a time: large print first, then the cream background, then the "no diagonal or backwards words" switch.
4. After that, a print button with an answer key would open the door to carers and teachers.
5. A small filter for unsuitable themes and accidental rude words in the grid can come next.
6. Finally, a short line of plain wording near the puzzle, with no health claims, plus a simple privacy notice.

**Stage 2: Put it to work for Growth Partner Pros (about 2 to 4 weeks)**

1. You might add one warm sentence under the puzzle, such as "Designed by Growth Partner Pros as an example of AI built for neurodivergent minds", with a link to your services.
2. It would be worth writing one short piece about the design choices: why no timer, why a gentle hint, why this font. That story is the consulting pitch.
3. You could use the puzzle live in your training sessions as a five-minute example of a small, humane AI build. Clients can type their own theme and see it work.
4. Then share it in the neurodivergent communities and newsletters you already belong to, and simply watch what people say. This also fills a research gap, since nobody found community feedback on this idea.

**Stage 3: Test paid demand cheaply, before building accounts (about 8 weeks, running alongside Stage 2)**

1. You could use your own generator to make one curated pack of large-print, calm-design puzzles, perhaps 20 puzzles on reminiscence themes for memory care, checking every word list by hand.
2. Then offer it for a small price, around $5 to $9, through a simple hosted checkout. No accounts are needed for this.
3. It would help to speak with five activity directors or special education teachers and ask what they use now and what they pay. Five conversations will tell you more than the research could.
4. Before going further, you can look up real search volumes in Google Keyword Planner, because the researchers could not get reliable numbers.

**Stage 4: Decide with evidence (after about 3 to 4 months in total)**

1. If the pack sells steadily and people ask for more, a small paid tier with saved collections and a group licence for care homes becomes worth building, with an independent security review before you accept payments.
2. If it does not sell, you have lost very little, and you still have a polished demonstration piece that keeps earning its place by bringing consulting conversations. On the evidence gathered, that second outcome is the more likely one, and it is still a good outcome.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. It began as "how do I make this generator commercially viable?" and the evidence answers that the generator is the commodity and your point of view is the scarce thing. Nobody in this market is visibly designing for calm, control and legibility, and nobody found is pairing a puzzle with real expertise in neurodivergent-friendly AI. That pairing is something only you bring, and a hundredth-of-a-cent puzzle that makes a prospective client think "she understands how my people think" is worth far more to a consulting business than a $5 subscription.

The gaps deserve the last word, because they are large. No one has measured willingness to pay for custom word searches in any segment; search volumes for the key terms are unknown; no user reviews or forum discussions were retrieved; no revenue figures exist for any comparable generator; and several legal topics (student privacy law, UK and Australian tax and privacy rules, detailed accessibility standards) were not researched at all. Those gaps are the reason the path above is built from small, cheap tests. Each step is designed to replace an assumption with something you have seen for yourself, at a pace that leaves room for your client work.
