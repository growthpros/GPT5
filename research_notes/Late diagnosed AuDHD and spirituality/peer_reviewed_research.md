# Peer-reviewed research on autism, ADHD, and co-occurring autism plus ADHD (AuDHD) in relation to religion, religiosity, belief, and spirituality (adult focus)

Direct page fetches from Wiley, Frontiers, NCBI, and Taylor & Francis were blocked in this session's network, so several non-PubMed items (Journal of Disability & Religion, Archive for the Psychology of Religion, International Journal for the Psychology of Religion, JSSR) are summarized from abstracts, indexes, or secondary descriptions. ## 1. Religiosity and belief in God among autistic people: the mentalizing-deficit hypothesis, replications, and critiques

### Takeaway
The claim that "autistic people are less religious" comes mainly from one 2012 paper (Norenzayan, Gervais & Trzesniewski) and one 2011 conference paper (Caldwell-Harris et al.). Later diagnosed-sample studies are mixed, and large general-population studies find that mentalizing or autistic-trait scores barely predict, or do not predict, belief. Cultural transmission (religious upbringing, "credibility-enhancing displays") predicts belief much better. The most recent narrative review (Burnos & Kopacz 2025) concludes that the mentalizing hypothesis "has not been unequivocally supported." What does show up fairly consistently is that autistic adults are more likely to be **non-affiliated or to build their own idiosyncratic belief systems**, and report **more, not fewer, spiritual or unusual experiences** in some samples.

### Cited Findings

**Foundational "less religious" studies**
- **[AUTISM, adults] Caldwell-Harris, Murphy, Velazquez & McNamara (2011)**, "Religious belief systems of persons with high functioning autism," *Proceedings of the 33rd Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society* (conference proceedings, not a journal article). Part 1 was a content analysis of religion threads on two US online forums (192 autistic vs 195 neurotypical posters). Part 2 was an online survey of 61 people with "high-functioning autism" (HFA) vs 105 neurotypical controls using AQ, SQ, and RMET. HFA participants were less likely to affiliate with traditional religions and more likely to identify as atheist or agnostic or to build a personal belief system. AQ scores correlated with religious belief. Limits: no control variables, and no detailed data on religious behavior or belief strength. — [eScholarship PDF](https://cloudfront.escholarship.org/dist/prd/content/qt6zh3j3pr/qt6zh3j3pr.pdf); design details and critique from [Burnos & Kopacz 2025, PMC full text](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- Secondary reposts of the forum data put HFA posters at roughly 26% atheist and 17% agnostic. This is a secondary source, so the figures should be checked against the PDF. — [GreaterWrong repost](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/RNSYDitY6688rP3Fb/atheism-and-the-autism-spectrum)
- **[AUTISM adolescents + TRAITS-GENERAL POP adults] Norenzayan, Gervais & Trzesniewski (2012)**, "Mentalizing deficits constrain belief in a personal God," *PLoS ONE* 7(5): e36880. Four studies:
  - Study 1: autistic adolescents expressed less belief in God than matched neurotypical controls.
  - Studies 2–4: in a Canadian student sample and two US national samples, autism-spectrum traits (AQ) predicted lower belief in God, and mentalizing (measured as the Empathy Quotient) mediated the effect. Systemizing, Conscientiousness, and Agreeableness did not mediate.
  - Mentalizing also mediated the gender gap in belief (men lower).
  - Doi: [10.1371/journal.pone.0036880](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0036880). — [PubMed 22666332](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22666332)
- Critical detail: Study 1, the only study with **diagnosed** autistic participants, had **11 autistic vs 13 neurotypical Florida teenagers, mostly male**. Belief was measured with four statements, and mentalizing was measured only indirectly (EQ). — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- Studies 3 and 4 (US adult samples) had N = 706 and N = 452. Autism still predicted lower belief after controlling for IQ (Study 1) and education (Studies 3–4). — [PLoS ONE article page](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0036880) (via search summary)
- Burnos & Kopacz note that a related Gervais & Norenzayan finding on analytic thinking and religiosity later failed to replicate. That is context for how fragile this research line is, not a replication of the mentalizing paper itself. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)

**Studies in diagnosed autistic adults that do not support, or only partly support, the hypothesis**
- **[AUTISM, young adults] Reddish, Tok & Kundt (2016)**, "Religious cognition and behaviour in autism: The role of mentalizing," *International Journal for the Psychology of Religion* 26(2): 95–112, doi: 10.1080/10508619.2014.1003518.
  - Sample: 19 HFA vs 19 neurotypical participants in Singapore, controlling for gender, age, IQ, religious upbringing, and affiliation.
  - Groups differed on mentalizing only on an advanced task, not on first- or second-order false-belief tasks.
  - There were "very little differences" in religious cognition and behavior across seven dimensions (belief strength, anthropomorphism, closeness to God, prayer habits, and others). Neurotypicals found prayer more attractive.
  - The relationship between mentalizing and most religion measures was "weak and negative."
  - Conclusion: mentalizing deficits have "only minimal impact" on how HFA adults think about gods.
  - — [Masaryk Univ. record](https://www.ics.muni.cz/en/research/publications/1316052); sample size from [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- **[AUTISM + self-identified traits, adults] Ekblad & Oviedo (2017)**, "Religious cognition among subjects with Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD): Defective or different?" *Clinical Neuropsychiatry* 14(4): 287–296.
  - Online survey via the "Aspie Quiz" site, N ≈ 2,000+ (806 with ASD traits vs 1,332 controls; about 38% autistic or with autistic traits, much of it **self-identified**).
  - Higher autistic traits went with **more** spiritual and paranormal experiences.
  - Autistic respondents reported **higher** religious practice than neurotypicals and more often built personal belief systems. The authors argue that developmental and sociocultural factors matter more than theory of mind.
  - — [ResearchGate listing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311900234_Religious_cognition_among_subjects_with_Autism_Spectrum_Disorder_ASD_Defective_or_different); details from [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- **[AUTISM adolescents + adults] Oviedo et al.** (two studies, cited in Burnos & Kopacz 2025):
  - Study 1: 11 autistic vs 421 neurotypical Spanish adolescents (Murcia).
  - Study 2: 318 Aspie Quiz adults, mean age 31, 70% autistic, mostly self-diagnosed.
  - Interest in supernatural experiences was comparable across groups. Autistic participants trusted videos of angelic interventions less.
  - Among autistic adolescents, interest in the supernatural was independent of religious practice.
  - — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692) (primary citation not retrieved)

**Image-of-God studies in Dutch clinical samples (Protestant mental-health settings)**
- **[AUTISM, adult outpatients] Schaap-Jonker, van Schothorst-van Roekel & Sizoo (2012)**, *Tijdschrift voor Psychiatrie* 54(5): 419–428 (Dutch, English abstract). Outpatients at a Protestant mental-health institution: more autistic traits went with greater fear of God and fewer positive feelings toward God. Strict Calvinists reported more fear of God. — [PubMed 22588956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22588956)
- **Schaap-Jonker et al. (follow-up, English-language)**: 78 Dutch adult ASD outpatients vs 240 psychiatric controls vs 459 non-clinical controls, using the Questionnaire God Image (QGI) and AQ-NL.
  - The ASD group's God image had fewer positive feelings and less perceived support, but negative feelings did not outweigh positive ones, and faith remained important to participants.
  - Social difficulties predicted anxiety toward God. People with high autistic traits who rated religion as highly important more often saw God as a "judge."
  - — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692) (exact journal and year of this paper not retrieved; likely *International Journal for the Psychology of Religion*, about 2013, so verify)
- **[AUTISM, adults] van Nieuw Amerongen-Meeuse et al.**: 103 respondents aged 17–63 at the Dimence clinic, Netherlands (42 ASD, 20 anxiety disorder, 41 non-clinical).
  - Religiosity was lower in ASD.
  - God image was shaped mainly by personality traits (low self-directedness and low reward dependence went with a negative God image).
  - Unlike the earlier Schaap-Jonker study, this one found **no** significantly more negative God image in ASD.
  - — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- Related: van der Velde, Schaap-Jonker et al. (2021), *J Nerv Ment Dis* 209(10): 710–719 (Dutch psychiatric patients n = 136, ASD included per MeSH terms, vs nonpatients n = 161). God-representation types tracked personality organization and Christian orthodox culture. — [doi:10.1097/NMD.0000000000001363](https://doi.org/10.1097/NMD.0000000000001363)

**General-population and trait studies (not diagnosed samples)**
- **[TRAITS-GENERAL POP] Maij et al. (2017)**, "Mentalizing skills do not differentiate believers from non-believers, but credibility enhancing displays do," *PLoS ONE* 12(8): e0182764. Five cross-cultural studies, over 67,000 participants in total.
  - The Empathy Quotient and AQ did not predict belief in supernatural agents in the Netherlands, Switzerland, or the US (a curvilinear effect appeared in the US).
  - Exposure to credibility-enhancing displays (seeing religious behavior during upbringing) was a strong predictor.
  - This is the largest direct test, and it is a failure to replicate the AQ-to-belief link.
  - — [PMC5568287](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5568287); [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0182764](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0182764)
- **[TRAITS-GENERAL POP] Lindeman, Svedholm-Häkkinen & Lipsanen (2015)**, *Cognition* 134: 63–76, N = 2,789, structural equation modeling. Mentalizing abilities had only **weak** relationships with religious, paranormal, and purpose beliefs. "Core ontological confusions" were the best predictor. — [doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2014.09.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2014.09.008)
- Lindeman & Svedholm-Häkkinen (2016), commentary in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 39: e18: the evidence for mentalizing as a basis of religious belief is "indirect, contradictory, and weak." — [doi:10.1017/S0140525X15000485](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X15000485)
- Supportive evidence for the opposing view, from Norenzayan's group: Willard & Norenzayan (2013), *Cognition* (N = 492 and N = 920), found that mentalizing predicted belief in God via dualism and teleology. — [doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2013.07.016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2013.07.016). White, Willard, Baimel & Norenzayan (2021), *Cognitive Science* (N = 2,006 and N = 1,752; Canada, India, US, Singapore), found that mentalizing, dualism, teleology, and intuitive thinking predicted belief in God and karma. — [doi:10.1111/cogs.12935](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12935)
- **[TRAITS-GENERAL POP] Crespi, Dinsdale, Read & Hurd (2019)**, "Spirituality, dimensional autism, and schizotypal traits: The search for meaning," *PLoS ONE*. Large undergraduate sample, AQ plus Hardt Spirituality Questionnaire.
  - Total spirituality correlated negatively with AQ, driven mainly by the "Search for Meaning" subscale.
  - Positive schizotypy correlated positively with spirituality.
  - **Belief in God was uncorrelated with autistic traits** but correlated with positive schizotypy.
  - — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0213456](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0213456)
- **[TRAITS-GENERAL POP] Smith, Yang, Doesburg & Crespi (2026)**, "Losing one's religion: relationships between autistic and schizotypal traits, religiosity, spirituality, and faith changes," *Frontiers in Psychology*, N = 2,091, religiously diverse.
  - People reporting a life-changing religious or spiritual experience, or a gain in faith, were lower in autistic **social** traits and higher in positive schizotypy.
  - Loss of faith was associated with higher positive schizotypy, not with autistic traits.
  - "No change in faith" was associated with higher autistic **imaginative** traits.
  - — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1689818](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1689818)
- **[TRAITS-GENERAL POP] Pyasik et al. (2026)**, *Acta Psychologica*. An experimental interoception manipulation (heartbeat-discrimination task with induced priors) increased **implicit spirituality but not religiosity**, and the size of the effect depended on autistic personality traits. This is an early mechanistic study. — [doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107185](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107185)
- Dutton and colleagues proposed that autistic traits partly explain the negative IQ–religiousness association. Dutton & Kirkegaard (2021), *J Religion & Health*, re-tested this with individual-level data (4,462 US Vietnam veterans) and found the IQ–belief gap is a Jensen effect (on g), contrary to the earlier autism-traits interpretation. This is a contested, ideologically charged literature and weak evidence on autism specifically. — [doi:10.1007/s10943-021-01351-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10943-021-01351-1)

**Reviews**
- **Burnos & Kopacz (2025)**, "Religiosity of adults on the autism spectrum: a cognitive and empirical analysis," *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 16: 1594692. Narrative review of nine empirical studies of autistic adults. The picture is "complex and marked by inconsistency." The central mentalizing hypothesis "has not been unequivocally supported." It is unclear whether autistic people are more, equally, or less religious. No study has directly tested the "broken mirror" (mirror neuron) hypothesis. — [doi:10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692) / [PMC12259667](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12259667/)
- **Kéri (2023)**, "Autism and Religion," *Children* (Basel) 10(8): 1417. Cognitive models of ASD (theory of mind, broken mirror, central coherence) overlap with models of religious belief, but the review does not conclude that ASD predicts low religiosity, and it emphasizes social and cultural factors. — [doi:10.3390/children10081417](https://doi.org/10.3390/children10081417)
- **Hayat et al. (2024)**, "The Intersection of Faith and Neurodiversity: Unraveling Religiosity in Autistic Adolescents," *Cureus*. Systematic search, 13 articles, 39 themes. Main finding: inconsistent terminology across studies. Adolescent-focused. — [doi:10.7759/cureus.61271](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.61271)

### Inferences
- The popular "autistic people are less religious or more atheist" claim rests on (a) one diagnosed sample of 24 mostly male teenagers, (b) a self-selected online forum and survey, and (c) AQ trait correlations in student and panel samples. The largest test (Maij et al., N > 67,000) did not replicate the AQ-to-belief link. This is **weak-to-moderate, contested evidence**, not an established fact.
- The more robust pattern across sources is **heterogeneity and individualization**: lower affiliation with institutional religion, more self-built belief systems, and in some samples equal or higher spiritual or unusual experiences. For a late-diagnosed adult, "less conventional," not "less spiritual," fits the evidence better.
- The Dutch clinical studies suggest that **where** autistic adults are religious (especially in strict Reformed settings), anxious or judging God images may be more common. The second Dutch study did not replicate this, which points to personality and culture as confounds.
- Crespi's work suggests autism traits may relate more to the "search for meaning" style of spirituality than to belief in God per se, though this is trait-level, student data.

### Gaps
- No preregistered replication of Norenzayan's mediation model in a **clinically diagnosed adult** sample was found.
- Full text of Caldwell-Harris (2011), Reddish et al. (2016), and Ekblad & Oviedo (2017) could not be opened, so exact effect sizes are not reported here.
- The exact citation of the English-language Schaap-Jonker 78-patient study and the van Nieuw Amerongen-Meeuse God-representation study was not retrieved (only via review).
- No studies found with large diagnosed samples of autistic **women** or late-diagnosed adults specifically.

## 2. Qualitative studies of how autistic adults experience and define spirituality

### Takeaway
Qualitative work, mostly small (n ≈ 9–17), Swedish, UK, and Christian-congregation samples, consistently finds that autistic adults can have rich, meaningful spiritual lives, often routed through **sensory experience, imagination or narrative immersion, pattern-seeking or logic, and relationships with "bodiless" agents**. These relationships can feel easier than human social interaction. The main barriers are social and sensory (worship environment, unpredictability, masking, neuronormative expectations of the "ideal churchgoer"), not an absence of belief capacity. Note: the person the brief calls "Ingrid Visuri" is **Ingela Visuri** (Södertörn University, Sweden).

### Cited Findings
- **[AUTISM, young adults] Visuri, I. (2018)**, "Rethinking Autism, Theism, and Atheism: Bodiless Agents and Imaginary Realities," *Archive for the Psychology of Religion* 40(1): 1–31, doi: 10.1163/15736121-12341348. Interviews with high-functioning autistic young adults. Two hypotheses:
  - (1) Communicating with bodiless agents (gods, angels, spirits) may be **less demanding** than peer interaction, because there is no body language, facial expression, or tone to decode.
  - (2) Retreat into "imaginary realities" is valued for predictability, emotional coherence, and benevolent minds.
  - Results do not support the assumption that autistic minds are poorly suited to experiencing supernatural agents.
  - — [RelBib record](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1576258106); summary also in [Kéri 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/children10081417)
- **[AUTISM, young adults] Visuri, I. (2020)**, "Sensory supernatural experiences in autism," *Religion, Brain & Behavior* 10(2), doi: 10.1080/2153599X.2018.1548374. In-depth study of 17 HFA young adults (Swedish) who identified as religious or spiritual. They described **many more** sensory supernatural incidents than a matched non-autistic group. The paper links this to research on unusual somatosensory experience in autism and to the "intersection between autistic embodiment and encultured cognition." — [RelBib](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1741048427/Details); [Södertörn dissertation PDF (DiVA)](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1317524/FULLTEXT01.pdf)
- Visuri, I. (2019), *Varieties of Supernatural Experience: The Case of High Functioning Autism* (PhD dissertation, Södertörn University; same 17 Swedish participants). Later expanded into the book *Autism and the Supernatural: Experiential Dimensions of Religiosity, Spirituality and Imagination* (Equinox, Advances in the Cognitive Science of Religion series). The book argues that autistic people experience invisible others through sensory perception, narrative immersion, imaginative practice, and embodied experience. — [Södertörn](https://www.sh.se/kalender/arkiv/2019-06-07-disputation-varieties-of-supernatural-experience-the-case-of-high-functioning-autism); [Equinox](https://equinoxreligionlibrary.com/projects/autism-and-the-supernatural)
- Burnos & Kopacz's description of Visuri's findings:
  - An imbalance between cognitive and emotional empathy.
  - Invisible beings perceived as "coherent and less complex."
  - Supernatural relationships resembling imaginary companions, offering "kindness, predictability, and opportunities to practice social skills in a safe environment."
  - — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- **[AUTISM, adults 16–55] van Ommen, A. L. & Endress, T. (2022)**, "Reframing Liturgical Theology Through the Lens of Autism: A Qualitative Study of Autistic Experiences of Worship," *Studia Liturgica* 52(2), doi: 10.1177/00393207221111573. Semi-structured online interviews (March 2020–May 2021) with 13 UK autistic Christian churchgoers without intellectual disability. Themes: worship experience, community, and encountering God.
  - Comfort came from community openness and service predictability.
  - Discomfort came from loss of control, unexpected stimuli, social anxiety, loud music, critical sermons, and sensory issues (smell, sound, touch).
  - Participants noted ableist liturgical texts, pattern recognition, and sensitivity to logical inconsistencies in doctrine.
  - Autistic traits were described as **both barriers and facilitators** of connection with God.
  - Church culture strongly shaped engagement.
  - — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00393207221111573); [Aberdeen portal](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/reframing-liturgical-theology-through-the-lens-of-autism-a-qualit/); details from [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- Related Aberdeen project materials: 21 autistic participants (13 speaking interviewees; UK, Singapore, US). Predictability helps manage sensory stress. This is non-peer-reviewed practice guidance. — [Aberdeen PDF](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/media/site/dhpa/4.0-How-can-churches-support-autistic-members.pdf)
- **[AUTISM, adults] "Work and Spirituality Among People With Asperger Syndrome: An Exploratory Study"** (2019), *Journal of Disability & Religion* 23(2), doi: 10.1080/23312521.2019.1580174. Interpretative phenomenological study of 12 adults on spirituality, fear of death, and colleague relationships at work. The abstract indicates spiritual concerns were minor relative to relational difficulties at work. Author names not retrieved. — [T&F abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/23312521.2019.1580174)
- **[AUTISM, nonverbal] Andersen, K. (2019)**, "Spirituality in the Context of Nonverbal Autism: A Research Process," *Journal of Disability & Religion* 23(4), doi: 10.1080/23312521.2019.1682104. Participants with severe nonverbal autism "revealed a sophisticated and deeply considered spirituality." — [T&F](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23312521.2019.1682104). An earlier scoping review on spirituality in nonverbal autism notes "little empirical research." — [RelBib](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1572019697)
- **[AUTISM, adults] "Autistic thinking, scripture and church: a qualitative case study and proposal"** (about 2021). Case study of one church with 9 adult autistic worshippers, on how their approach to the Bible differs from their church's. The authors note most church pastoral-care resources focus only on children. Journal not confirmed. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354080627_Autistic_thinking_scripture_and_church_a_qualitative_case_study_and_proposal)
- **[AUTISM vs non-autistic] "Autistic and Non-Autistic Experience of the Sensory Aspects of the Church Service"** (about 2024, quantitative). Autistic people experienced significantly more sensory barriers in services and received or required more accommodations. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382755546_Autistic_and_Non-Autistic_Experience_of_the_Sensory_Aspects_of_the_Church_Service)
- **[AUTISM, adult psychiatric patients] van Nieuw Amerongen-Meeuse et al. (2023)**, "Varieties of Religious and Spiritual Struggles by Type of Mental Disorder: A Qualitative Approach," *Psychopathology*. 34 semi-structured interviews across six diagnostic groups in Dutch clinical care. "Patients with autism mentioned doubts and troubles with religious beliefs." — [doi:10.1159/000531027](https://doi.org/10.1159/000531027)
- **[AUTISM, adults] Love, Cai, Edwards & Gibbs (2023)**, *Autism in Adulthood*, brief report, N = 111, co-produced with autistic researchers. Religious identity was **not** associated with autism-disclosure decisions or outcomes (sexual identity was). — [doi:10.1089/aut.2023.0005](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2023.0005)
- **[AUTISM, adults] Cameron et al. (2021)**, systematic review of community participation in autistic adults (63 reports, 58 studies), *J Applied Research in Intellectual Disabilities*. **Religious groups** were identified as one form of community participation. — [doi:10.1111/jar.12970](https://doi.org/10.1111/jar.12970)
- Not peer-reviewed, but relevant:
  - Kent doctoral narrative study: all participants struggled to meet the "neuronormative behavioural standards of the 'ideal churchgoer.'" — [KAR](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/107678)
  - Jung (2025), Claremont undergraduate thesis on autistic adults in Christian and Jewish spaces: participants valued relief from confusing social rules and comfort amid sensory overload, and "may leave communities more quickly when these needs go unmet." — [Claremont](https://scholarship.claremont.edu/scripps_theses/2698)
  - An autoethnography highlights **masking or passing** as central to (lack of) belonging in congregations. — via search summary only, no stable link retrieved
- **Theoretical or phenomenological papers** (not empirical):
  - Narzisi & Muccio (2021), *Brain Sciences*: hypersensitivity and reduced reliance on cognitive priors may predispose autistic people to spiritual experience understood as present-moment awareness, not religious belief. — [doi:10.3390/brainsci11070914](https://doi.org/10.3390/brainsci11070914)
  - Vidt (2026), *J Pastoral Care & Counseling*: an essay by a parent and chaplain reframing spirituality in severe autism as embodied and sensory. — [doi:10.1177/15423050261420829](https://doi.org/10.1177/15423050261420829)
- **Attitudes of congregations** (not autistic participants): Waldock & Forrester-Jones (2020), *Journal of Disability & Religion*, exploratory interviews with 21 church-going Christians in South East England about attitudes toward autism. — [Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/an-exploratory-study-of-attitudes-toward-autism-amongst-church-go/)

### Inferences
- Across Visuri, van Ommen & Endress, and the Andersen nonverbal work, the recurring definition of autistic spirituality is **embodied or sensory, imaginative, pattern-based, and relational with non-human or bodiless agents**, rather than primarily social or communal. The common difficulty is with the social and sensory **container** of religion, not with belief itself.
- The masking, belonging, and "ideal churchgoer" findings fit the late-diagnosis literature on camouflaging. That is an inference: no study found links late diagnosis to religious experience directly.
- This evidence is **qualitative and small-n**. It is good for describing experiences, not for estimating prevalence.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed qualitative study found that focuses specifically on **late-diagnosed** autistic adults and spirituality.
- Studies "Jacobs et al." and "Kenny-related work" named in the brief could not be identified. No matching peer-reviewed autism–spirituality paper by those authors surfaced, so they may not exist as described.
- No qualitative studies found in *Autism* or *Autism in Adulthood* whose primary focus is spirituality in autistic adults. The relevant literature sits mainly in theology, religious-studies, and cognitive-science-of-religion journals.
- Non-Christian adult perspectives (Muslim, Jewish, Hindu, Buddhist, secular, or pagan autistic adults) are nearly absent. A UK Jewish community IPA study (n = 10) surfaced but its findings were not retrievable.
- The Springer reference entry "Autistic People, Religion, and Spirituality" (doi 10.1007/978-3-031-40858-8_400-1) exists, but its author and content could not be retrieved. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-031-40858-8_400-1)

## 3. ADHD and religion or spirituality

### Takeaway
The ADHD literature is small, mostly quantitative, and mostly secondary analyses of existing datasets (US Add Health; Israeli online panels; an international ADHD+SUD cohort). The consistent pattern: **ADHD symptoms are associated with lower public or organized religious engagement** (attendance, community participation), less consistently with private practice. **Religiosity, especially observance, is associated with fewer ADHD symptoms and may buffer some negative outcomes**, but in some US analyses religiosity *interacted* with ADHD to predict worse outcomes. Everything is correlational. No qualitative studies of ADHD adults' spiritual experience were found.

### Cited Findings
- **[ADHD, adolescents] Dew, Daniel & Koenig (2007)**, "A pilot study on religiousness/spirituality and ADHD," *International Journal of Adolescent Medicine and Health* 19(4): 507–510, doi: 10.1515/ijamh.2007.19.4.507. 117 adolescent psychiatric outpatients, Brief Multidimensional Measure of Religiousness/Spirituality. **No significant relation** between R/S dimensions and ADHD diagnosis. — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/702030)
- **[ADHD, adolescents to adults] Dew, Kollins & Koenig (2020/2022)**, "ADHD, Religiosity, and Psychiatric Comorbidity in Adolescence and Adulthood," *Journal of Attention Disorders*. Over 8,000 Add Health participants aged 12–34 across four waves.
  - ADHD symptoms correlated with **lower levels of all religious variables at nearly all waves**.
  - At Wave IV (adulthood), prayer and attendance **interacted** with ADHD to predict **worse** psychopathology.
  - The authors ask whether lower religiosity partly explains ADHD comorbidities (depression, delinquency, substance use).
  - — [doi:10.1177/1087054720972803](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054720972803)
- **[ADHD, adults] Upenieks & Fishman (2025)**, "Paths of Faith and Focus? ADHD, Adult Religiosity, and Moderation by Race in the Add Health Study," *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion*, doi: 10.1111/jssr.70089.
  - Childhood ADHD diagnosis was linked to **lower adult religious service attendance, but not to private practices like prayer**.
  - The attendance gap was larger for Black and Asian American adults.
  - Full text not opened. The finding comes from news coverage and the publisher page; one web search could not confirm the paper's details, so verify before citing specifics.
  - — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jssr.70089); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/childhood-adhd-diagnosis-linked-to-lower-adult-religious-attendance/)
- **[ADHD, Add Health, about 4,156]** "Pray the ADHD Away? An Analysis of the Intergenerational Nexus of Religion, Symptoms, and Diagnosis" (UTSA repository): higher religiosity went with fewer ADHD symptoms but was unrelated to diagnosis. Authors and peer-review status not confirmed. — [UTSA](https://rrpress.utsa.edu/items/6625cbbd-4f20-472a-b1aa-52aeec4838a1)
- **[ADHD, adults] Novis-Deutsch, Dayan, Pollak & Khoury-Kassabri (2021)**, "Religiosity as a moderator of ADHD-related antisocial behaviour and emotional distress among secular, religious and Ultra-Orthodox Jews in Israel," *International Journal of Social Psychiatry*. N = 806 adults.
  - Religiosity correlated with lower ADHD symptoms and diagnosis, and directly with less antisocial behavior and distress.
  - It **moderated (buffered)** the negative effects of ADHD.
  - Religious **observance** drove the buffering. Religious **belonging** drove **negative attitudes toward ADHD**, which is a stigma signal.
  - — [doi:10.1177/00207640211005501](https://doi.org/10.1177/00207640211005501)
- **[ADHD + SUD, adults] Begeman, Schellekens, van den Brink, Barta et al. (2025)**, "Religiousness and self-control in patients with substance use disorder and comorbid ADHD," *International Journal of Social Psychiatry*. International cohort, N = 578 treatment-seeking adults. Higher religiousness was associated with **lower ADHD severity** (not SUD severity), and this was **not mediated by self-control**. — [doi:10.1177/00207640251331973](https://doi.org/10.1177/00207640251331973)
- **[ADHD, adults] Dayan et al. (2023)**, *Criminal Behaviour and Mental Health*, N = 2,025 Israeli adults. Religiosity was included as a covariate in ADHD–antisocial-behavior models. It is not a focal finding. — [doi:10.1002/cbm.2288](https://doi.org/10.1002/cbm.2288)
- **[ADHD, college students] Figueroa et al. (2019)**, *J Ethnicity in Substance Abuse*, N = 1,053. Religiosity was directly associated with self-efficacy to avoid prescription-stimulant misuse, and was protective for White students but a risk factor for Latinx students. — [doi:10.1080/15332640.2018.1542370](https://doi.org/10.1080/15332640.2018.1542370)
- **[ADHD, classification] Bölte et al. (2024)**, *Developmental Medicine & Child Neurology*. The revised WHO ICF Core Sets for ADHD **added "religion and spirituality"** as a participation domain after stakeholder input, which formally recognizes it as part of lived functioning. — [doi:10.1111/dmcn.15865](https://doi.org/10.1111/dmcn.15865)
- **[ADHD, CHILD/FAMILY, brief]**
  - Kaya Kara et al. (2023), Turkey, 94 adolescents with ADHD vs 109 without: lower involvement in religious activities, with moderate-to-large effects. — [doi:10.1080/01942638.2023.2253899](https://doi.org/10.1080/01942638.2023.2253899)
  - Halstead et al. (2023), ALSPAC (UK): children of atheist-class mothers had higher ADHD odds (OR 1.41) vs agnostic, while highly or moderately religious mothers' children had more internalizing problems. — [doi:10.1017/S003329172300079X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S003329172300079X)
  - Budman (2025), qualitative study on ADHD recognition in an Ultra-Orthodox Jewish community. — [doi:10.1186/s12889-025-25522-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-025-25522-x)
  - A 2025 qualitative study of Sri Lankan mothers of children with ADHD found spiritual practice used as coping. — [PMC11697869](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11697869/)
- Lay beliefs: Park, Lee & Kim (2018), *Psychiatry Research*, N = 673 Korean adults: "volitive/religious" causes formed one factor of lay causal beliefs about ADHD, Tourette syndrome, and ASD. — [doi:10.1016/j.psychres.2018.08.023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychres.2018.08.023)
- A 2025 paper in *Journal of Spirituality in Mental Health*, "Spirituality, neurodiversity, and mental health: understanding unique spiritual expressions among neurodivergent populations," discusses autism and ADHD spiritual expression and integration into care. It appears conceptual; empirical content not verified. — [doi:10.1080/19349637.2025.2548539](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19349637.2025.2548539)

### Inferences
- The ADHD pattern (lower **organized** attendance, private practice relatively preserved) is consistent with executive-function and attention demands of scheduled, sit-still services, and with stigma in religious communities (the Israeli "belonging leads to negative attitudes" finding). This is an inference: the studies do not test mechanisms.
- The religiosity-and-fewer-symptoms association could reflect selection (people with fewer symptoms sustain practice), structure or self-regulation benefits, or community support. Begeman et al. found it was not explained by self-control. Direction of causality is unknown.
- The evidence base is **dataset-driven and Western or Israeli-Jewish**, with no lived-experience voice.

### Gaps
- **No qualitative peer-reviewed study** found of how adults with ADHD experience or define spirituality.
- Mindfulness and meditation for adult ADHD is a separate, sizable clinical-trial literature (mindfulness-based programs as secular interventions). It was **not** searched in detail in this session and should not be equated with spirituality. A report writer wanting it should verify primary RCTs and meta-analyses separately.
- "Faith-based ACT for ADHD" surfaced only as a conference session, not peer-reviewed. — [conference listing](https://worldconference.net/414-adhd-or-overwhelmed-using-faith-based-acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act-to-find-focus/)
- No studies found of meaning-in-life or spiritual-wellbeing scales specifically in diagnosed ADHD adults.

## 4. Research specifically on co-occurring autism and ADHD (AuDHD) and spirituality

### Takeaway
**No peer-reviewed empirical study was found that examines religion, religiosity, belief, or spirituality specifically in people with co-occurring autism and ADHD, adult or child.** Autism studies do not report ADHD comorbidity as a variable, and ADHD studies do not report autism. The AuDHD question is an evidence gap, not a weak-evidence area.

### Cited Findings
- Systematic PubMed searching combining autism, ADHD, and religion or spirituality terms found no AuDHD-specific study of spirituality. The only paper combining both diagnoses with religious or spiritual coping concerned **mothers** of children with autism or ADHD (Berenguer et al. 2024, latent profile analysis of coping), a **[CHILD/FAMILY]** study, not about the neurodivergent person's own spirituality. — [doi:10.1080/20473869.2024.2331748](https://doi.org/10.1080/20473869.2024.2331748)
- A web search for AuDHD and spirituality likewise found "no study that specifically examines adults with co-occurring autism and ADHD and their religion or spirituality"; available sources treat each condition separately or are general neurodiversity reviews. — search summary, citing the [J Spirituality in Mental Health 2025 paper](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/19349637.2025.2548539) as the closest general work
- The nine empirical adult-autism studies in the most recent review (Burnos & Kopacz 2025, Table 1) are described by diagnosis or trait groups, with no AuDHD subgroup analysis reported. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)

### Inferences
- Since about 50–70% of autistic adults in clinical samples also meet ADHD criteria (a widely cited range, not verified in this session), it is likely that **some participants** in existing autism samples (for example the Dutch outpatient samples or Aspie Quiz respondents) had undiagnosed or unreported ADHD. Existing autism findings may therefore partly reflect AuDHD people, but this cannot be separated out.
- Any claim about "AuDHD spirituality" must be built by **extrapolation** from autism-only and ADHD-only findings, and should be labeled as such. For example: sensory or imaginative routes to the sacred (autism literature) plus difficulty sustaining scheduled, organized attendance (ADHD literature).

### Gaps
- No studies of AuDHD and religion or spirituality, no studies of late-diagnosed AuDHD adults and spirituality, and no studies of how a late diagnosis (of either condition) reshapes religious identity or deconversion.
- Grey literature (community surveys, blogs, practitioner books) was out of scope and not assessed.

## 5. Spirituality as a coping resource or wellbeing factor for neurodivergent adults; intervention studies

### Takeaway
Direct evidence for neurodivergent adults themselves is thin but slightly positive. One longitudinal US study of 306 autistic adults found transcendent spirituality predicted greater psychological flexibility and life satisfaction over 10 weeks. One survey of 201 autistic adults found spiritual needs and practices, especially **secular practices such as mindfulness or meditation**, tied to quality of life, though religious needs were less pronounced than in other samples. In ADHD, religiosity correlates with fewer symptoms and buffers distress in Israeli adults. **No intervention trials** testing spiritual or religious interventions for autistic, ADHD, or AuDHD adults were found. Most "spirituality as coping" literature in autism is about **parents and caregivers**.

### Cited Findings
- **[AUTISM, adults] Kelley & Aller (2025)**, "Transcendent Spirituality and Psychological Flexibility Among Adults on the Autism Spectrum in the United States," *Journal of Religion and Health*. Two-wave longitudinal structural equation model, **N = 306 autistic adults**, 10 weeks.
  - Transcendent spirituality (feeling connected to a higher being) was longitudinally associated with **psychological flexibility and satisfaction with life**, but not with psychological inflexibility.
  - Psychological flexibility **mediated** the spirituality → life-satisfaction link.
  - This is the strongest adult wellbeing study found, though it is observational and short-term.
  - — [doi:10.1007/s10943-025-02448-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10943-025-02448-7)
- **[AUTISM, adults] Spiritual-needs survey, N = 201** (book chapter, Springer, doi: 10.1007/978-3-030-70139-0_20, in a 2021 volume on spiritual needs in research and practice).
  - Cross-sectional survey: autistic adults "generally have spiritual needs and use religious and spiritual practices," associated with several quality-of-life measures.
  - These needs and practices were less pronounced than in other samples.
  - Per a 2025 citing study, **secular spiritual practices (mindfulness, meditation) were important** for many, while religious needs and practices were less common.
  - Chapter authors not retrieved; this is a book chapter, not a peer-reviewed journal article.
  - — [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-70139-0_20)
- **[ADHD, adults]** Buffering evidence: Novis-Deutsch et al. 2021 (N = 806; observance moderates ADHD → distress and antisocial behavior) — [doi:10.1177/00207640211005501](https://doi.org/10.1177/00207640211005501). Begeman et al. 2025 (N = 578; religiousness associated with lower ADHD severity) — [doi:10.1177/00207640251331973](https://doi.org/10.1177/00207640251331973). Counter-evidence: Dew et al. 2020 (religiosity interacted with ADHD to predict worse adult psychopathology in some models) — [doi:10.1177/1087054720972803](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054720972803)
- **[ID / low-support-needs-excluded] Bertelli et al. (2020)**, *J Religion & Health*. Literature mapping found spirituality relevant to quality of life in intellectual disability and "low-functioning" ASD but scarcely investigated. — [doi:10.1007/s10943-019-00891-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10943-019-00891-x)
- **[AUTISM, adults] Koenig et al. (2025)**, *Autism*: interviews with 10 autistic adult and emergency-contact dyads. Autistic adults defined health as **subjective wellbeing** and challenged neurotypical norms. This is relevant context for wellbeing framing; spirituality is not a focal theme in the abstract. — [doi:10.1177/13623613251362336](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613251362336)
- **Intervention studies found (all [CHILD/FAMILY])**: Pandya (2021), *JADD*, WhatsApp-based spiritual posts to reduce stress and build resilience in **mothers** of autistic children. — [doi:10.1007/s10803-020-04633-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-020-04633-x)
- **[CHILD/FAMILY, brief]** A large caregiver literature reports religious and spiritual coping in parents of autistic children:
  - Ekas, Tidman & Timmons (2019), *JADD*: religiosity/spirituality → better maternal mental health, mediated by positive thinking. — [doi:10.1007/s10803-019-04165-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-019-04165-z)
  - Davis et al. (2024), *Autism Research* scoping review: spirituality is often endorsed by Black caregivers for acceptance and coping. — [doi:10.1002/aur.3243](https://doi.org/10.1002/aur.3243)
  - Iranian, Ghanaian, Palestinian-Israeli, Ugandan, and Vietnamese family studies 2022–2026, some reporting supernatural causal attributions and stigma:
    - Khadija et al. 2025, Ghana — [doi:10.1186/s12888-025-06844-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-025-06844-x)
    - Vincent et al. 2026, Uganda — [doi:10.1177/13623613261434571](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261434571)
    - Habiballah & Abu-Akel 2026 — [doi:10.1177/13623613261498497](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261498497)
    - Ha & Whittaker 2022, Vietnam — [doi:10.1080/09638288.2022.2040613](https://doi.org/10.1080/09638288.2022.2040613)
  - Khadija et al. note that religious engagement served as coping for autistic adolescents, but **sensory sensitivities posed participation challenges**.

### Inferences
- Kelley & Aller's mediation finding (spirituality → psychological flexibility → life satisfaction) is a plausible bridge to acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)-style supports. The 201-adult survey's emphasis on secular practices suggests that, for many autistic adults, "spirituality" as a wellbeing resource may look like contemplative or nature practice more than institutional religion.
- Given the qualitative barriers (sensory overload, unpredictability, masking, stigma), religious **community** participation could be a stressor for some neurodivergent adults even when belief or spirituality is a resource. The ADHD "worse outcome" interactions and the Dutch "anxious God image" findings are consistent with this (inference).

### Gaps
- **No randomized or controlled intervention study** of a spiritual, religious, or spiritually integrated intervention for autistic, ADHD, or AuDHD adults was found.
- No study tests whether faith communities' accommodations improve neurodivergent adults' wellbeing outcomes.
- No study of religious or spiritual struggle, religious trauma, or deconversion following late diagnosis.

## 6. Methodological critiques: theory-of-mind framing, deficit models, small or male-skewed samples, Western Christian bias

### Takeaway
The literature has well-documented weaknesses:
- Reliance on a contested theory-of-mind deficit framing.
- Very small diagnosed samples.
- Heavy use of AQ or EQ trait proxies in non-diagnosed samples.
- Self-identified online samples.
- Male-skewed or adolescent "high-functioning" samples.
- Inconsistent definitions of religiosity vs spirituality.
- An almost exclusively Western and Christian (especially Dutch Protestant and UK Anglican or evangelical) evidence base.
- Little or no participatory involvement of autistic or ADHD people (one exception: the Love et al. 2023 brief report).

### Cited Findings
- **Western and measurement bias**: Burnos & Kopacz (2025) state that most research "has been conducted from a Western perspective," uses "measurement tools grounded in Western traditions," and that this produces "an incomplete and potentially biased approach." They attribute the inconsistent results partly to "various operational definitions and measurements of religiosity, autism, and mentalizing." They call for centering autistic perspectives, exploratory and mixed methods, longitudinal designs, and non-Western samples and tools. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- **Small, male, adolescent samples**: Norenzayan et al.'s only diagnosed sample had 11 autistic vs 13 control mostly male teenagers. Reddish et al. had 19 vs 19. Oviedo's adolescent autism group had n = 11. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- Norenzayan et al. themselves tie the autism–belief link to the **male** gender gap in belief, which builds sex confounding into the hypothesis. — [PubMed 22666332](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22666332)
- **Proxy measures**: mentalizing was measured indirectly via the self-report Empathy Quotient in Norenzayan et al. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- Lindeman & Svedholm-Häkkinen (2016) call the overall evidence for mentalizing as a basis of belief "indirect, contradictory, and weak." — [doi:10.1017/S0140525X15000485](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X15000485)
- Maij et al. (2017) show cultural learning (credibility-enhancing displays) outperforms mentalizing in samples over 67,000. — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0182764](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0182764)
- **Self-diagnosed online samples**: the Aspie Quiz samples (Ekblad & Oviedo; Oviedo et al. Study 2) were largely self-identified. Caldwell-Harris used self-selected forum posters. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- **Deficit model vs difference**: Ekblad & Oviedo frame the issue as "defective or different?" and urge exploring the diversity of autistic religiosity rather than testing deficit-derived predictions. Visuri explicitly challenges the cognitive-science-of-religion assumption that belief in invisible agents requires typical mindreading. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692); [RelBib, Visuri 2020](https://www.relbib.de/Record/1741048427/Details)
- The mirror-neuron ("broken mirror") account used in this literature "has faced substantial criticism and failed replications," and no study has directly tested it with religiosity. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692)
- **Terminology inconsistency**: Hayat et al.'s (2024) review of 13 studies found that the main finding was the variable terminology and features used to study "religiosity in autism." — [doi:10.7759/cureus.61271](https://doi.org/10.7759/cureus.61271)
- **Clinical-setting bias**: the Dutch God-image studies recruited from Protestant or Christian mental-health institutions, where Calvinist orthodoxy independently predicted fear of God. — [PubMed 22588956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22588956); [doi:10.1097/NMD.0000000000001363](https://doi.org/10.1097/NMD.0000000000001363)
- **ADHD literature**: largely secondary analyses of US Add Health (dominant religion Christian) and Israeli-Jewish panels, all correlational. Effects vary by race and ethnicity (Upenieks & Fishman 2025; Figueroa et al. 2019), which warns against generalizing. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jssr.70089); [doi:10.1080/15332640.2018.1542370](https://doi.org/10.1080/15332640.2018.1542370)

### Inferences
- Evidence strength ranking:
  - **Strongest**: large general-population trait studies (Maij, Lindeman, White, Smith/Crespi), but these are about traits, not diagnosed autistic people.
  - **Moderate**: Kelley & Aller (N = 306 diagnosed or self-identified autistic adults, longitudinal); the Dutch clinical God-image studies (N = 42–78 ASD); the Israeli and Add Health ADHD analyses (large but correlational, with indirect ADHD measures).
  - **Weak but rich**: qualitative work (Visuri n = 17; van Ommen & Endress n = 13).
  - **Weakest for the "less religious" claim**: Norenzayan Study 1 (n = 24) and Caldwell-Harris (self-selected forum).
- Late-diagnosed adults, women, and nonbinary people (a large share of the late-diagnosed AuDHD population) are essentially **unrepresented**. The literature's typical subject is a young, male, "high-functioning," Western Christian-background participant (inference from sample descriptions).

### Gaps
- Sex and gender breakdowns of most samples could not be verified because full texts were inaccessible.
- No study used participatory or co-produced methods focused on spirituality, apart from the general identity brief report (Love et al. 2023).
- No meta-analysis of autism and religiosity exists; the reviews found (Burnos & Kopacz 2025; Kéri 2023; Hayat 2024) are narrative or descriptive.
