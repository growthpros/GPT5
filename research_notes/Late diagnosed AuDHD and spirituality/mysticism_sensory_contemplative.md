# Mysticism, Sensory Experience, and Contemplative Practice in Neurodivergent (Autistic, ADHD, AuDHD) Adults

Research notes. Scope: adults; literature through Nov 2026. Evidence labels are used throughout:
- **[EMPIRICAL]** = data-based study (with design/sample noted)
- **[THEORY]** = conceptual or theoretical model, not directly tested on spirituality
- **[COMMUNITY/PRACTITIONER]** = lived-experience, blog, clinician-opinion, or popular-press claims
- **[PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE]** = well-known citation that could not be re-opened this session (web fetch to tandfonline.com, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and researchsquare.com was blocked by the session's network). ## 1. Theories and evidence linking autistic perception to spiritual, mystical, or awe experience (monotropism, intense world, sensory intensity, pattern perception, Grandin, awe, absorption)

### Takeaway
There is no single tested theory of "autistic mysticism"; instead there are adjacent theories (monotropism/autistic flow, Intense World, sensory hypersensitivity, mentalizing-based cognitive science of religion) and a small, recent empirical base. The best new evidence (a 2026 preprint, n≈192) finds autistic adults experience awe at least as much as non-autistic adults, with differences in time perception, physiology, and the *sources* of awe; older cognitive-science-of-religion work found autistic traits predict somewhat weaker belief in a *personal God*, mediated by mentalizing, but qualitative work shows many autistic people have rich, often sensory-based, religious experiences.

### Cited Findings

**Monotropism and autistic flow [THEORY]**
- Monotropism (Dinah Murray, Mike Lesser, Wenn Lawson) proposes that autistic attention tends to be pulled strongly into a small number of interests at a time ("attention tunnels"), rather than distributed across many. Original statement: Murray, D., Lesser, M., & Lawson, W. (2005). Attention, monotropism and the diagnostic criteria for autism. *Autism*, 9(2), 139–156. [PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE; doi 10.1177/1362361305051398] — [monotropism.org](https://monotropism.org/?p=62)
- Fergus Murray (autistic theorist, runs monotropism.org) is especially interested in the role of flow states in autistic learning and wellbeing — [Middletown Centre for Autism podcast](https://buzzsprout.com/1184276/episodes/15538261-podcast-monotropism-with-fergus-murray); [monotropism.org](https://monotropism.org/?p=94)
- Heasman, B., Williams, G., Charura, D., Hamilton, L. G., Milton, D., & Murray, F. (2024). "Towards autistic flow theory: A non-pathologising conceptual approach." *Journal for the Theory of Social Behaviour*. Argues autistic flow may qualitatively diverge from traditional (Csikszentmihalyi) flow models and proposes flow as a non-pathologising lens on autism. — [Portsmouth research portal](https://researchportal.port.ac.uk/en/publications/towards-autistic-flow-theory-a-non-pathologising-conceptual-appro/); [Kent repository](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/106162/)
- A British Psychological Society *Psychologist* article on autism in therapy explicitly links monotropism, meditation and autistic flow, quoting a practitioner (Lowry) that when autistic people are in monotropic flow "they truly are in a meditative state," with a claimed alpha-to-theta brainwave shift. This brainwave claim is a practitioner assertion, not cited data. [COMMUNITY/PRACTITIONER] — [BPS Psychologist](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/autism-therapy-monotropism-meditation-and-autistic-flow)
- I found no source in which Fergus Murray or Dinah Murray explicitly frames monotropism as a theory of spiritual/mystical experience; the spiritual extension is made by others. — [search summary, monotropism sources](https://monotropism.org/?p=62)

**Intense World Theory and sensory intensity [THEORY]**
- Intense World Theory (Kamila and Henry Markram) proposes autism involves hyper-reactive, hyper-plastic local microcircuits producing hyper-perception, hyper-attention, hyper-memory and hyper-emotionality, with withdrawal as a secondary protective response. Markram, H., Rinaldi, T., & Markram, K. (2007). The intense world syndrome. *Frontiers in Neuroscience*; Markram, K., & Markram, H. (2010). The intense world theory – a unifying theory of the neurobiology of autism. *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 4:224. [PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE; doi 10.3389/fnhum.2010.00224]
- Favre, Markram & Markram (2019) restate Intense World Theory (drawing on the valproic-acid rodent model) as a framework for individual differences in sensory sensitivity, using signal/noise concepts. Note the core evidence base is largely animal-model. — [Cogn Neurosci, PubMed; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/17588928.2019.1592143)
- Atypical sensory sensitivity is shared between autism and synaesthesia: on the Glasgow Sensory Questionnaire, both autistic people and synaesthetes reported increased sensory sensitivity vs controls with a large effect size, and synaesthetes scored in the autistic range on AQ "attention to detail." [EMPIRICAL] — [Ward et al. 2017, Sci Rep, PubMed; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep41155)
- Inference link to spirituality (Intense World → awe/mysticism) is not tested in any study I found.

**Absorption [THEORY + general-population EMPIRICAL]**
- Lifshitz, van Elk & Luhrmann (2019) review: trait absorption (Tellegen Absorption Scale) predicts spiritual/mystical experience and the intensity of awe and self-transcendence; they propose absorption as a mechanism linking attention/imagination to spiritual experience. Not autism-specific. — [Consciousness and Cognition (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1053810018305257); [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334328123_Absorption_and_spiritual_experience_A_review_of_evidence_and_potential_mechanisms_A_R_T_I_C_L_E_I_N_F_O)
- I found **no** published study administering the Tellegen Absorption Scale to diagnosed autistic or ADHD adults in relation to spiritual experience (see Gaps).

**Awe in autistic adults [EMPIRICAL, preprint]**
- Srinivasan, H., Yaden, D. B., & Keltner, D. (posted March 2026). "The Architecture of Awe: Experience, Disposition, and Meaning-Making in Autism." Research Square preprint (not peer reviewed). Multi-method study; recruited 100 autistic and 100 neurotypical adults via academic listservs and an online pool; analyzed n = 93 autistic, 99 neurotypical; autistic group included formally diagnosed and self-identified. Across momentary experience, dispositional measures and narratives, autistic adults did **not** show reduced awe. Differences: altered time perception and physiological aspects of awe, and narratives emphasizing different interpersonal and abstract sources of awe. (Details from abstract/search summaries; full text not accessible.) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/402528124_The_Architecture_of_Awe_Experience_Disposition_and_Meaning-Making_in_Autism); [Research Square PDF](https://assets-eu.researchsquare.com/files/rs-9142343/v1_covered_c454c7cc-4666-4521-a3ba-7e83c3570d8c.pdf)
- Notable: the author Hari Srinivasan is himself autistic; David Yaden is a Johns Hopkins psychedelic/religious-experience researcher; Dacher Keltner is the leading awe researcher. (Affiliation facts are general knowledge [PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE].)

**Sensory supernatural experiences [EMPIRICAL, qualitative + small comparison]**
- Visuri, I. (2019/2020). "Sensory supernatural experiences in autism." *Religion, Brain & Behavior*. Swedish study of autistic young adults (n = 17); participants reported sensing presences, touch, or visions without a physical source; 14 of 17 reported at least one such experience; reports were more frequent than matched non-autistic controls. Visuri argues these experiences are sensory-based and meaningful, pushing back on the idea that autistic people lack religious experience because of mentalizing differences. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/2153599X.2018.1548374); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330407475_Sensory_supernatural_experiences_in_autism)
- Clark, K. J., & Visuri, I. (2019). "Autism and the panoply of religious belief, disbelief and experience" — review arguing the autism–religion relationship is diverse, not uniformly atheistic. — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/15220847/Clark_K_J_and_Visuri_I_2019_Autism_and_the_panoply_of_religious_belief_disbelief_and_experience); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337086582_Autism_and_the_Panoply_of_Religious_Belief_Disbelief_and_Experience)

**Mentalizing account of belief (cognitive science of religion) [EMPIRICAL + THEORY]**
- Norenzayan, Gervais & Trzesniewski (2012), *PLOS ONE*, "Mentalizing Deficits Constrain Belief in a Personal God": higher autism-spectrum scores predicted weaker belief in God (β = −.13, p = .02, controlling for gender), and mentalizing mediated this; religious attendance did not explain it. Note: this concerns belief in a *personal, agentic* God, not spiritual experience generally. — [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0036880)
- Follow-up work by Norenzayan's group frames mentalizing and intuitive thinking as part of the explanation for individual differences in religious belief, but stresses cultural learning; belief in non-agentic karma follows partly different cognitive pathways than belief in God. — [White, Baimel & Norenzayan 2020, Curr Opin Psychol; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2020.07.033); [White et al. 2021, Cogn Sci; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12935)
- Caldwell-Harris et al. (2011) used content analysis of online posts and questionnaires and is widely credited with drawing attention to an autism–atheism link (more atheism/agnosticism and self-constructed belief systems among autistic respondents). Later studies have not uniformly replicated the autism–atheism association. — [Clark & Visuri review summary](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/337086582_Autism_and_the_Panoply_of_Religious_Belief_Disbelief_and_Experience); [Religious Cognition and Behaviour in Autism: The Role of Mentalizing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/273092730_Religious_Cognition_and_Behaviour_in_Autism_The_Role_of_Mentalizing)
- A related Gervais & Norenzayan (2012) result linking analytic thinking to lower religiosity failed to replicate (noted in secondary review). — [MDPI Religions 2025, "Autism Spectrum Disorders, Anxiety, and Religion"](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/3/371)
- A 2025 study ("Religiosity of adults on the autism spectrum: a cognitive and empirical analysis") tested predictions of lower belief strength, less anthropomorphic deity concepts, lower perceived prayer effectiveness, and less preference for spontaneous prayer in autistic adults, plus interviews with 13 autistic Christians; full results could not be retrieved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393412347_Religiosity_of_adults_on_the_autism_spectrum_a_cognitive_and_empirical_analysis)
- A quantitative study of 201 adults with ASD found they generally have spiritual needs and use religious/spiritual practices, though these needs and practices were less pronounced than in comparison samples. — [Spiritual Needs of People with ASD (PDF)](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Jan-Cwik/publication/344725281_Spiritual_Needs_of_People_with_Autism_Spectrum_Disorder/links/6149cbf3a595d06017de9978/Spiritual-Needs-of-People-with-Autism-Spectrum-Disorder.pdf)
- A 2025 reference-work chapter, "Autistic People, Religion, and Spirituality," exists (Springer). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-031-40858-8_400-1)
- van Ommen & Endress (2022, *Studia Liturgica*): 13 in-depth interviews with autistic people about worship; themes: experience of worship, community, and encountering God; argue autistic worshipers are indispensable to the church and question liturgical "normalcy." [EMPIRICAL, qualitative] — [search summary via Springer chapter listing](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-031-40858-8_400-1)

**Temple Grandin on belief [COMMUNITY/autobiographical]**
- Grandin's *Thinking in Pictures* (1995) contains a chapter titled "Stairway to Heaven: Religion and Belief"; secondary sources say she was raised Episcopalian, moved away from belief in a personal God toward a scientific outlook, and describes God as an ordering force in the universe. Exact wording not verified from the book. — [Wikiwand: Temple Grandin](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Temple_Grandin); [blog quoting autobiography](https://rtraer.blogspot.com/2021/02/sharing-self-forgetting-is-like-glimpse.html)
- Search found no reliable source for claims that Grandin ties belief to quantum entanglement or makes specific afterlife claims (frequently repeated online). — [search result set](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Temple_Grandin)

**Pattern perception / crossmodal priors [EMPIRICAL, relevant to "seeing patterns/meaning" claims]**
- Pre-registered study (31 autistic vs 37 non-autistic, IQ-matched): autistic participants made fewer theoretically congruent crossmodal matches across four tasks (e.g., "fast lemons," sound-size, colour-taste), suggesting a broad reduction in shared crossmodal priors rather than heightened associative/symbolic linking. — [Król et al. 2026, Autism; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261441750)

### Inferences
- The strongest current empirical picture is *not* "autistic people are less spiritual" but "autistic people may be less drawn to anthropomorphic, socially mediated concepts of God, while experiencing awe and sensory-based transcendent experiences at comparable or higher rates." The mentalizing literature and the awe/sensory literature are measuring different things (belief in a personal agent vs. experience).
- Monotropism + autistic flow theory offers a plausible bridge to contemplative absorption (single-pointed attention resembles concentration meditation), but this is theoretical; nobody has empirically tested monotropic flow against mystical-experience scales.
- The Król 2026 finding cautions against assuming autistic people perceive *more* symbolic/associative connections; "pattern recognition" in autism is better framed as detail/system-based than as enhanced intuitive association.

### Gaps
- No study found administering the Tellegen Absorption Scale, Hood Mysticism Scale, or MEQ30 (outside psychedelic contexts) to diagnosed autistic or ADHD adults.
- No study of awe or mystical experience in ADHD or AuDHD adults specifically.
- The awe preprint is unreviewed; effect sizes and measures (e.g., which dispositional awe scale) could not be read.
- Visuri 2019 control-group details and exact frequencies beyond 14/17 not verified (full text blocked).

## 2. ADHD, hyperfocus, flow, and spiritual experience; novelty-seeking and spiritual seeking

### Takeaway
Hyperfocus is reliably reported more by ADHD adults and overlaps only modestly with flow; it has both adaptive and maladaptive sides. Adults with ADHD score higher on Cloninger's novelty seeking (temperament) and self-transcendence (character, which includes spiritual acceptance/transpersonal identification) in several controlled samples, which is the closest empirical hint of an ADHD–spiritual-seeking link; no study directly measures ADHD hyperfocus as a route to mystical experience.

### Cited Findings
- Ashinoff & Abu-Akel ("Hyperfocus: the forgotten frontier of attention," *Psychological Research*, online 2019 / print 2021) review hyperfocus and propose it and flow are essentially the same construct given shared features; secondary sources note they distinguish being "in the zone." — [summarized in Frontiers in Psychiatry 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.886692/full); [RUG thesis summary](https://gmwpublic.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/1181/1/BachelorThesis_ADHD_Hyperfocus_LTMWellinkvoorde_S4004264.pdf)
- Hupfeld, Abagis & Shah (2019) developed the Adult Hyperfocus Questionnaire; adults with (self-reported) ADHD reported higher hyperfocus than those without. [EMPIRICAL] — [summary in Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.886692/full); [Univ. of Michigan Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/handle/2027.42/169422/quynht.pdf?sequence=1)
- In adults with ADHD, AHQ hyperfocus scores showed only low-to-moderate correlations with the Dispositional Flow Scale-2, suggesting related but non-identical constructs. [EMPIRICAL] — [Frontiers in Psychiatry 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2022.886692/full)
- Qualitative interviews with seven UK adults with ADHD (Bowen & Horlin, per secondary summaries) found hyperfocus complex, variable and distinct from flow, with both adaptive and maladaptive features. [EMPIRICAL, qualitative; secondary report] — [RUG thesis](https://gmwpublic.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/1158/1/Final%20version%20thesis%20-%20ADHD%20and%20Hyperfocus%20-%20B.B.%20Leenders.pdf)
- A 12-item dispositional AHQ (AHQ-D) was validated in 347 adults (mean age 33; 47% female) (Hupfeld et al. 2024, per secondary summary). — [Scientific Reports (DOAJ record)](https://doaj.org/article/4fafdcea90614363aedc3cc0d95cf462)
- Personality: 119 adults with ADHD vs 403 controls (Temperament and Character Inventory): high novelty seeking, harm avoidance and **self-transcendence**, and low self-directedness and cooperativeness, were associated with ADHD diagnosis. [EMPIRICAL] — [Personality profiles in adults with ADHD (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4908674/)
- A controlled study (206 ADHD vs 123 non-ADHD adults) found ADHD adults endorsed more novelty seeking, harm avoidance and self-transcendence, and less reward dependence, persistence, self-directedness and cooperativeness. [EMPIRICAL] — [J Atten Disord reference listing](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.1177/1087054715613440)
- Smalley et al. (2009, *J Clin Psychol*, "Mindfulness and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder") reported adults with ADHD were less mindful than controls, more novelty seeking, less self-directed and more self-transcendent, and suggested mindfulness might raise self-directedness. [EMPIRICAL; attribution to Smalley is PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE] — [PMC2827240](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2827240)
- Caveat: in Cloninger's model, self-transcendence is a *character* dimension (with self-directedness, cooperativeness), not temperament; novelty seeking is the only temperament trait consistently elevated. — [Focus/PsychiatryOnline review](https://focus.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/foc.8.2.foc269)

### Inferences
- Elevated TCI self-transcendence in ADHD is the most direct quantitative signal that ADHD adults may be more open to transpersonal/spiritual experience; combined with novelty seeking, it fits a "spiritual seeker / tradition-hopping" pattern often described in communities, but no study measures spiritual seeking behavior (e.g., conversion, multiple traditions) in ADHD.
- Hyperfocus may function as a doorway to absorption-like states, but because it overlaps only modestly with flow and has maladaptive features (time loss, neglect of needs), it should not be equated with contemplative concentration.

### Gaps
- No study found on ADHD and mystical experience scales, religious conversion, or spiritual "seeking."
- Original Hupfeld 2019 and Ashinoff & Abu-Akel full texts not accessed; dates/details come from secondary summaries.
- No AuDHD-specific data on hyperfocus vs monotropic flow.

## 3. Mindfulness and meditation for autistic and ADHD adults; adverse effects (Britton/VCE) and relevance to neurodivergent or trauma-affected people

### Takeaway
For autistic adults, a small evidence base (Spek et al. 2013 RCT; later trials and a 2025 network meta-analysis) suggests adapted mindfulness reduces anxiety, depression and rumination, with mindfulness-based interventions ranking highest for anxiety among non-drug options. For ADHD adults, meta-analyses show small-to-medium benefits on symptoms vs inactive controls, but effects shrink to low/negligible against active controls (e.g., Hoxhaj 2018 found mindfulness no better than psychoeducation). Meditation adverse effects are real and under-reported (Britton's VCE study); there is no dedicated study of meditation harms in neurodivergent adults, though sensory and interoceptive differences make body-scan and breath practices plausible friction points.

### Cited Findings

**Autistic adults [EMPIRICAL]**
- Spek, van Ham & Nyklíček (2013), *Research in Developmental Disabilities* 34(1):246–253: 42 "high-functioning" autistic adults randomized to 9-week MBT-AS (modified MBCT: no metaphors, more concrete instruction, longer program) or wait-list; intervention group had significant reductions in depression, anxiety and rumination and increased positive affect vs controls. Authors called for larger trials and follow-up. — [PubMed 22964266](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22964266/); [Tilburg repository](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/23d39c5f-7776-4980-9694-2aca888612bf); [AIDE Canada summary](https://aidecanada.ca/learn/communication/mindfulness-therapy-helps-adults-with-autism-spectrum-disorders) (DOI 10.1016/j.ridd.2012.08.009)
- Ding et al. (2025) network meta-analysis of 67 RCTs (3,604 autistic participants): mindfulness-based interventions ranked best for anxiety (SMD = −0.84, 95% CI −1.32 to −0.36; SUCRA 91.4%); CBT best for depression; physical activity best for quality of life. Stronger effects in adults and with 9–16-week interventions. Samples mainly male and without intellectual disability. — [Front Psychiatry; DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1660412)
- A 6-week smartphone-based mindfulness RCT vs waitlist in autistic adults with anxiety (73 completers in intervention) has a 2026 secondary analysis on predicting responders; response varied considerably across individuals. Prevalence context given: 20–65% of autistic adults have at least one anxiety disorder. — [Ahn et al. 2026, JMIR AI; DOI](https://doi.org/10.2196/89054)
- EASE (Emotion Awareness and Skills Enhancement), a mindfulness-based program developed specifically for autistic people (Mazefsky group), has a comparative-effectiveness RCT protocol vs the Unified Protocol in autistic youth/young adults, and an EEG study (n = 39) showing neural changes in theta/beta power after EASE vs active control. — [Mazefsky et al. 2025 protocol, BMC Psychol; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-03891-7); [Susam et al. 2026, IEEE TNSRE; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/TNSRE.2026.3698744)

**ADHD adults [EMPIRICAL]**
- Zylowska et al. (2008), *J Atten Disord* 11(6):737–746: feasibility study of the 8-week Mindful Awareness Practices (MAPs) for ADHD in 24 adults and 8 adolescents; most completed and reported high satisfaction; pre-post improvements in self-reported ADHD symptoms, attention and cognitive-inhibition tasks, anxiety and depression; no control group. — [UMN Experts](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/mindfulness-meditation-training-in-adults-and-adolescents-with-ad/); [Qigong Institute abstract](https://qigonginstitute.org/abstract-print/4061)
- Mitchell et al. (pilot RCT; online 2013): adults with ADHD randomized to 8-week group mindfulness (n = 11) or waitlist (n = 9); large-effect improvements in self- and clinician-rated ADHD and executive-function symptoms and emotion dysregulation; no improvement on lab EF tasks. — [J Atten Disord; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054713513328)
- Mitchell, Zylowska & Kollins (2015) review the rationale and MAPs approach for adult ADHD. — [Cogn Behav Pract; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cbpra.2014.10.002)
- Hoxhaj et al. (2018), *Eur Arch Psychiatry Clin Neurosci*: first RCT vs an active control; 81 medication-free adults with ADHD in 8-week MAP vs structured psychoeducation; both groups improved on observer-rated inattention (partial η² = 0.18) with gains maintained at 6-month follow-up; **no difference between groups**; women benefited more than men overall; men showed the largest changes under MAP. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-018-0868-4)
- Bachmann et al. (2018) randomized fMRI study (21 MAP vs 19 psychoeducation completers) on working memory in adult ADHD. — [Behav Res Ther; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2018.05.002)
- Oliva et al. (2021) meta-analysis/meta-regression (31 studies, children and adults): in adults, medium pooled effect on ADHD symptoms but some publication bias; **medium/large effects vs inactive controls but low/negligible vs active controls**; MAPs and MBCT most used in adults; recommended as complement, not replacement. — [J Affect Disord; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2021.05.068)
- Kim & Jung (2025) meta-analysis (10 controlled trials, adults): significant improvements in self-reported ADHD symptoms (SMD 0.48), observer-rated symptoms (SMD 0.32) and functioning (SMD 0.56); no significant effects on mindfulness skills, negative affect or positive affect. — [Medicine (Baltimore); DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000044308)
- Xue et al. (2019) meta-analysis (11 studies, 682 participants, mixed ages) reported large effects on inattention (g = −0.825) and hyperactivity/impulsivity (g = −0.676) vs controls. (Earlier, less conservative estimate.) — [Medicine (Baltimore); DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000015957)
- López-Pinar et al. (2020) meta-analysis of psychotherapies for comorbid internalizing symptoms in adult ADHD: significant between-group effect for mindfulness-based therapies only on self-esteem; CBT had the broadest support. — [J Atten Disord; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054719855685)
- Large 2025 syntheses including mindfulness among adult-ADHD interventions: Ostinelli et al. component NMA (Lancet Psychiatry) and Gosling et al. umbrella review (BMJ). Specific mindfulness estimates were not extracted. — [Ostinelli et al. 2025; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(24)00360-2); [Gosling et al. 2025, BMJ; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2025-085875)

**Adverse effects of meditation [EMPIRICAL + THEORY]**
- Lindahl, Fisher, Cooper, Rosen & Britton (2017), *PLOS ONE*, "The varieties of contemplative experience: A mixed-methods study of meditation-related challenges in Western Buddhists": qualitative interviews with Western Buddhist meditators and teachers investigating challenging, distressing or functionally impairing experiences. The project identified 50+ experience types across seven domains: somatic, affective, cognitive, perceptual, conative, sense of self, and social. Specific numbers in my prior knowledge (60 practitioners; 59 experience categories; 26 categories of influencing factors across practitioner, practice, relationship and health-behavior domains) need verification. — [PLOS ONE via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5443484); [Britton Lab project overview](https://sites.brown.edu/britton/?p=221); [Cheetah House VCE](https://cheetahhouse.org/vce)
- Lindahl et al. (2019, Oxford Handbook of Meditation) argue for a person-centred approach to telling normal contemplative development apart from adverse effects/psychopathology and note most scientific research does not report adverse effects. — [chapter PDF](https://sites.brown.edu/britton/files/2023/10/Lindahl-2019-Oxford-Handbook-of-Meditation-chapter.pdf)
- A separate Lindahl 2017 *Religions* paper addresses "somatic energies and emotional traumas" in Vajrayāna practitioners (content not retrieved). — [search summary](https://sites.brown.edu/britton/?p=221)
- Popular coverage of Britton's work (Brown Alumni Magazine, Jan 2025) on meditation side effects; Cheetah House offers support for meditators in difficulty. [secondary] — [Brown Alumni Magazine](https://www.brownalumnimagazine.com/articles/2025-01-16/side-effects-of-meditation); [Psychology Today "duty to warn"](https://psychologytoday.com/us/blog/therapy-and-buddhist-traditions/202206/duty-warn-about-meditation-sickness)
- Farias et al. (2020, *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica*) systematic review of 83 studies reported meditation can trigger adverse effects (e.g., anxiety, depression); my prior knowledge is an overall prevalence ≈8.3%. Reached only through a secondary article this session. [PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE for the figure] — [The Swaddle (secondary)](https://www.theswaddle.com/why-meditation-works-for-some-people-but-not-others)
- UK resource collating research evidence on meditation safety. — [Meditating in Safety](https://meditatinginsafety.org.uk/category/research-evidence/)

**Relevance to neurodivergent/trauma-affected people**
- I found **no** peer-reviewed survey of meditation adverse effects specifically in autistic or ADHD adults; neurodivergent-specific harm claims come mainly from practitioner articles and blogs. — [search result assessment](https://www.mindful.org/learning-to-celebrate-neurodiversity-in-mindfulness/)
- Mindful.org piece (drawing on CAMH Azrieli Adult Neurodevelopmental Centre experience): neurodiverse people report sensory experiences that can produce adverse responses to body scan, breath practices and loving-kindness; many describe themselves as mindfulness "drop-outs." [COMMUNITY/PRACTITIONER] — [Mindful.org](https://www.mindful.org/learning-to-celebrate-neurodiversity-in-mindfulness/)
- Blog claim that still, breath-focused practice "fails" for autistic and ADHD adults and movement-based alternatives work better. [COMMUNITY, opinion] — [Medium: "The Still Mind Paradox"](https://medium.com/unheard-voices/the-still-mind-paradox-why-meditation-fails-for-autistic-and-adhd-adults-and-what-works-instead-c65e72b75a29)
- ADHD–PTSD comorbidity: systematic review of 21 adult studies reports increased PTSD risk among people with ADHD (relevant because trauma history is a known VCE influencing factor for difficult meditation experiences). — [Magdi et al. 2025, Syst Rev; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-025-02774-7)
- Interoceptive mismatch in autism (see Section 4) gives a mechanistic reason body-focused practices could raise anxiety: autistic adults show lower heartbeat-detection accuracy but inflated self-reported body sensitivity, and the gap correlates with anxiety. — [Garfinkel et al. 2016, Biol Psychol; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2015.12.003)

### Inferences
- For autistic adults, the evidence supports *adapted* (concrete, literal, metaphor-light, sensory-aware) mindfulness for anxiety/rumination; standard protocols with heavy metaphor and long silent body scans were explicitly modified in MBT-AS.
- For ADHD adults, mindfulness is a reasonable complement but not clearly superior to good psychoeducation; the "large effect" headline mostly reflects waitlist controls.
- Given (a) elevated trauma/PTSD rates in ADHD, (b) interoceptive mismatch and sensory sensitivity in autism, and (c) VCE showing trauma and practice intensity shape difficult experiences, late-diagnosed AuDHD adults plausibly face elevated risk of difficult meditation experiences, especially on intensive retreats; this is inferred, not directly measured.

### Gaps
- No RCT of mindfulness specifically in AuDHD (co-occurring) adults.
- No dedicated study of meditation-related adverse effects prevalence in autistic or ADHD samples.
- Spek 2013 sample count appears as 40 in some summaries vs 42 randomized in the abstract (use 42 randomized).
- Ostinelli 2025 and Gosling 2025 mindfulness-specific effect estimates not extracted.

## 4. Adapted practices: movement, stimming as embodied/sacred practice, special interests as devotion, nature-based spirituality, ritual/routine as sacred structure, interoception

### Takeaway
Direct research on adapted *spiritual* practices for neurodivergent adults is nearly absent; evidence exists for the building blocks (stimming and special interests serve positive regulatory and connective functions; physical activity improves quality of life in autism; interoceptive training reduces anxiety in autistic adults), while the framing of stimming as prayer, special interests as devotion, or routine as sacred ritual is a community/theological claim, not tested.

### Cited Findings
- Stimming (qualitative, 15 autistic adults, 2026): stimming is not only private coping but also a way of connecting, sharing feelings and communicating ("Safety, Connection, and Communication"). [EMPIRICAL] — [Bates et al. 2026; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/25739581261424784)
- Survey of autistic adults ("Beyond self-regulation," 2025): most reported stimming is positive at some times and negative at others; functions go beyond self-regulation. [EMPIRICAL] — [Morris et al. 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/27546330241311096)
- Autistic adolescents and young adults describe stimming and special interests as "not harmful" and as serving positive functions that continue into adulthood. [EMPIRICAL, qualitative] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/380606465_They_Are_Not_Harmful_Autistic_Adolescents_and_Young_Adults'_Experiences_and_Views_of_Stimming_and_Special_Interests)
- No study links stimming or special interests to prayer or devotional practice; the intersection is unstudied. — [search assessment; closest is van Ommen & Endress worship study via Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-031-40858-8_400-1)
- Repetitive prayer (rosary) qualitative study (general population, not neurodivergent): described calming and meditative effects, relevant by analogy to repetitive/rhythmic practice. — [Experiences and Perceived Effects of Rosary Praying (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8542536/)
- Movement: in the 2025 autism NMA, physical activity ranked best for quality of life (SMD 0.59, 95% CI 0.20–0.98). [EMPIRICAL] — [Ding et al. 2025; DOI](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1660412)
- Interoception: ADIE (Aligning Dimensions of Interoceptive Experience) RCT in 121 autistic adults (61 ADIE, 60 active control): six sessions of heartbeat-detection training with feedback plus exercise to raise cardiac arousal reduced trait anxiety at 3 months (mean difference 3.23; d ≈ 0.30); 31% met recovery criteria vs 16% control; one adverse event (cardiac anxiety). Low reliance on language and emotional insight. [EMPIRICAL] — [Quadt et al. 2021, EClinicalMedicine; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eclinm.2021.101042)
- Interoceptive awareness moderates emotional prosody recognition in autistic adults. [EMPIRICAL] — [Mulcahy et al. 2019, Biol Psychol; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2019.05.011)
- Autistic adults show reduced interoceptive accuracy plus exaggerated interoceptive sensibility; the discrepancy ("trait prediction error") correlates with emotion-sensitivity deficits and anxiety. [EMPIRICAL] — [Garfinkel et al. 2016; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2015.12.003)
- Reviews of interoception's dimensions and role in emotion. [THEORY/REVIEW] — [Greenwood & Garfinkel 2024, Annu Rev Psychol; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-020924-125202); [Quadt, Critchley & Garfinkel 2018; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/nyas.13915)
- Monotropic flow framed as meditative state (practitioner claim) supports special-interest immersion as a contemplative analogue. [COMMUNITY/PRACTITIONER] — [BPS Psychologist](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/autism-therapy-monotropism-meditation-and-autistic-flow)
- Neurodiversity-affirming mindfulness adaptations (eyes open, movement, choice of anchor, avoiding forced body scan) are described in practitioner literature. [COMMUNITY/PRACTITIONER] — [Mindful.org](https://www.mindful.org/learning-to-celebrate-neurodiversity-in-mindfulness/)

### Inferences
- Interoceptive training (ADIE) is the best-evidenced "body-awareness" approach for autistic adults and differs from classic body-scan by giving objective feedback; this suggests contemplative practices that calibrate body awareness with feedback may suit autistic people better than open-ended inward attention.
- Stimming and special interests have documented positive regulatory and connective functions; framing them as embodied or devotional spiritual practice is a reasonable extrapolation for communities, but should be presented as interpretation.
- Ritual/routine as "sacred structure" is consistent with autistic preference for predictability, but I found no study testing it.

### Gaps
- No empirical study of nature connection / nature-based spirituality in autistic or ADHD adults surfaced in this session's searches (searches did not specifically return any; this remains an evidence gap or a search gap).
- No study of ritual/routine as spiritual structure in neurodivergent adults.
- No trials of movement-based contemplative practice (yoga, walking meditation, tai chi, qigong) specifically in autistic or ADHD adults with spiritual outcomes.

## 5. Synesthesia, hyper-empathy, and claims about intuition or "sensitivity": evidence vs claims

### Takeaway
Evidence supports higher synaesthesia rates in autism (strongest in savant subgroups) and shared sensory hypersensitivity, but it does **not** support claims that mirror-touch synaesthesia gives heightened empathy, and recent data show autistic people share *fewer* conventional crossmodal associations. Claims of enhanced intuition, psychic sensitivity, or "empath" status in neurodivergent spiritual communities have no supporting empirical evidence that I found.

### Cited Findings
- Baron-Cohen et al. (2013), *Molecular Autism* 4:40, "Is synaesthesia more common in autism?": synaesthesia rate ≈18.9% in autistic adults vs ≈7.2% in controls (self-report plus consistency testing). [EMPIRICAL; PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE figures] — DOI 10.1186/2040-2392-4-40 (not re-verified)
- Hughes et al. (2017): grapheme-colour synaesthesia was significantly more prevalent in autistic people **only** in those with savant skills (n = 40 savant autistic, 34 non-savant autistic, 29 controls), suggesting the link is with savant abilities rather than autism per se. [EMPIRICAL] — [Multisens Res; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1163/22134808-00002558)
- Ward et al. (2017): synaesthetes and autistic people share elevated sensory hyper- and hypo-sensitivity (large effect). [EMPIRICAL] — [Sci Rep; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep41155)
- Baron-Cohen et al. (2016): largest mirror-touch sample (46 pure MT, 36 grapheme-colour, 46 controls): MT synaesthetes did **not** show enhanced empathy; 30% of all 135 MT synaesthetes recruited reported an autism diagnosis; MT synaesthetes had higher autistic trait scores; some MT responses occurred to objects being touched. [EMPIRICAL] — [PLoS One; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0160543)
- Rare variants in axonogenesis genes found in three families with sound-colour synaesthesia; authors note synaesthesia appears more common in autism and savant abilities. [EMPIRICAL, genetic] — [Tilot et al. 2018, PNAS; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1715492115)
- Autistic adults made fewer conventional crossmodal correspondences (pre-registered, 31 vs 37), which argues against a general "enhanced associative intuition." [EMPIRICAL] — [Król et al. 2026, Autism; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261441750)
- Interoceptive accuracy is reduced in autism while self-reported body sensitivity is inflated, a relevant caution for claims that autistic people "sense energy" or are more bodily attuned. [EMPIRICAL] — [Garfinkel et al. 2016; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsycho.2015.12.003)
- Visuri's autistic participants reported sensory supernatural experiences (presences, touch, visions) more than controls; this documents the *experience*, not any paranormal accuracy. [EMPIRICAL, n = 17] — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330407475_Sensory_supernatural_experiences_in_autism)

### Inferences
- "Hyper-empathy" claims in autistic communities find only indirect support (sensory/affective intensity per Intense World Theory) and are contradicted for the mirror-touch route; the empirical case is better framed as intense affective reactivity plus differences in cognitive empathy, not superior empathic accuracy.
- Experiences of sensed presence or heightened sensory "messages" can be validated as real experiences while staying agnostic on their source; synaesthesia and sensory hypersensitivity are documented phenomena that may underlie some reports.

### Gaps
- No systematic study of "empath," "highly sensitive," or intuitive/psychic self-identification among autistic or ADHD adults in spiritual communities.
- No data on synaesthesia prevalence in ADHD or AuDHD from this session.
- Baron-Cohen 2013 figures not re-verified.

## 6. Psychedelic research and autistic adults (brief)

### Takeaway
Evidence is limited to self-selected surveys and a small MDMA pilot trial: most autistic psychedelic users attribute reduced distress and social anxiety to an impactful experience, about 20% report undesirable effects, and accounts include mystical features (unity, transcendence, awe); controlled trials are scarce and the broader psychedelic-therapy evidence is being critically re-examined.

### Cited Findings
- Stroud et al. (2024), *Psychopharmacology* 242(2):373–387 (UCL, co-produced with the Autistic Psychedelic Community): online survey of 233 self-selected autistic adults with high AQ scores on their most impactful psychedelic experience (mostly LSD/psilocybin). 82% attributed reduced psychological distress, 78% reduced social anxiety, 70% increased social engagement; ~20% reported undesirable effects such as increased anxiety, some describing the experience among the most negative of their lives. Only substantial predictor of distress reduction: increased psychological flexibility. Authors caution about non-experimental design and biased sampling. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00213-024-06685-8); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10198948)
- Accounts in that survey described profound unity, transcendence, ineffability and awe (per secondary coverage). — [PsyPost](https://psypost.org/psychedelic-use-linked-to-reduced-distress-increased-social-engagement-in-autistic-adults)
- Afsharnia et al. (2025), *PLOS Mental Health* (CAMH Toronto): 261 autistic adults completed an online survey; 77.8% willing to try psychedelics for mental health; 69.7% reported past use (mostly psilocybin mushrooms); higher doses and highly meaningful experiences correlated with longer-lasting perceived mental-health improvements; barriers were legal, health and logistical. Self-selecting sample. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000514)
- Reviews note ongoing studies of MDMA for social anxiety in autistic adults. — [Baldo 2024, Arch Toxicol; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00204-024-03765-8)
- Danforth et al. (2018), *Psychopharmacology*: randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot of MDMA-assisted therapy for social anxiety in 12 autistic adults, with greater reductions in social anxiety vs placebo. [PRIOR-KNOWLEDGE; DOI 10.1007/s00213-018-5010-9, not re-verified]
- Scoping review: no completed psychedelic trials in under-18s; proposed trials include MDMA/psilocybin for autism with social anxiety. — [Rajwani et al. 2025, Lancet Child Adolesc Health; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S2352-4642(25)00208-1)
- Broader caution: a 2026 meta-analysis found psychedelic-assisted therapy for depression no more effective than open-label antidepressants under equal unblinding conditions (co-author Zachary J. Williams is an autism researcher). — [Williams, Barnett & Szigeti 2026, JAMA Psychiatry; DOI](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2025.4809)

### Inferences
- Psychedelic data echo the awe findings: autistic adults can have intense mystical-type experiences, but the evidence is retrospective and self-selected, and a meaningful minority report harm.

### Gaps
- King's College London (Declan Murphy / Grainne McAlonan group) psilocybin/LSD mechanistic studies in autistic adults and any Johns Hopkins autism-specific psychedelic studies were not retrieved this session; details unverified.
- No psychedelic data specific to ADHD or AuDHD adults retrieved.
