## 1. Autistic and ADHD adults' experiences in congregations: sensory barriers, social expectations, exclusion, and being labelled rebellious, lazy or spiritually deficient

### Takeaway
A small but growing body of mostly UK-based qualitative work, plus one quantitative sensory study, consistently finds the same things. Autistic adults face sensory and social barriers and ableist attitudes in churches. They are often dismissed when they ask for accommodations, and they belong only when their needs are actually met. Research rarely captures ADHD adults' own congregational experiences. The quantitative ADHD evidence shows markedly lower adult attendance, but not the reasons for it. Almost all of the evidence is Christian, and mostly Protestant or Anglican.

### Cited Findings
**Autism: qualitative and mixed-methods studies of adults**
- **[PR]** Waldock & Forrester-Jones (2020), *Journal of Disability & Religion*, interviewed 21 adult churchgoers (autistic and non-autistic) from four Protestant churches in England. Three themes emerged: "different understandings of autism", "degrees of ableism" and "idiosyncratic practice". — [Waldock & Forrester-Jones 2020, Univ. of Kent repository](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/82656/1/WRDH-2019-0028%20blinded%20article%20RESUBMISSION.pdf)
- **[PR, autoethnography]** Krysia Waldock (2023), "The Impossible Subject: Belonging as a Neurodivergent in Congregations," *Journal of Disability & Religion* 27(4): 568–583, DOI 10.1080/23312521.2023.2249452. Waldock is an autistic, disabled researcher with a Kent PhD on autistic people in religious groups.
  - Church leadership dismissed Waldock's concerns about noise. That left a choice between discomfort in the main building and exclusion from the community by sitting apart.
  - Waldock found belonging only when their needs were met and their autistic embodiment was recognized and valued.
  - The article frames prior research as showing that neurodivergent people often feel they don't belong in congregations, and that some have been stigmatized or excluded. — [Taylor & Francis PDF listing](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/23312521.2023.2249452); [Waldock ResearchGate profile](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Krysia-Waldock)
- **[PR]** van Ommen & Endress (2022), "Reframing Liturgical Theology Through the Lens of Autism: A Qualitative Study of Autistic Experiences of Worship," *Studia Liturgica*. It is based on 13 in-depth interviews with autistic people, with themes on worship, community and encountering God. The authors argue that autistic ways of experiencing worship are "mostly disregarded in practical and in liturgical theology." — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00393207221111573); [Aberdeen research portal](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/reframing-liturgical-theology-through-the-lens-of-autism-a-qualit/)
- **[PR]** "Autistic thinking, scripture and church: a qualitative case study and proposal" is a qualitative case study of nine adult autistic worshippers. Only the title and sample size were retrieved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/354080627_Autistic_thinking_scripture_and_church_a_qualitative_case_study_and_proposal)
- **[PR]** "Toward Accessible Worship: The Experiences and Insights of Christians with Disabilities," *Journal of Disability & Religion* 2024, 28(2): 189–219. A qualitative study of 37 adults with varied disabilities, including autistic participants, it identifies 15 dimensions of worship services that bear on accessibility. — [Baylor-hosted PDF](https://disability.research.baylor.edu/sites/g/files/ecbvkj1796/files/2024-11/toward_accessible_worship_the_experiences_and_insights_of_christians_with_disabilities_1.pdf)

**Autism: the quantitative sensory study**
- **[PR]** Unwin & van Ommen (2024), "Autistic and Non-Autistic Experience of the Sensory Aspects of the Church Service." Search summaries describe it as the first quantitative study of sensory barriers to worship for autistic people.
  - Autistic participants reported significantly more sensory barriers ("Barriers," Component 1). They also received or needed more accommodations ("Accommodations," Component 3).
  - Autistic participants "felt more connected to God when in control of sensory and social aspects of church."
  - All participants, autistic or not, enjoyed some sensory aspects of the service.
  - The authors call the results preliminary. They recommend autism-informed accommodations as universal design that benefits the whole congregation. — [DOI 10.1177/00916471241266810](https://doi.org/10.1177/00916471241266810); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/00916471241266810)
  - **Venue conflict:** one search summary placed this paper in the *Journal of Disability & Religion*. The SAGE DOI prefix (0091-6471) matches the ISSN of the *Journal of Psychology and Theology*, so treat JPT as the likely venue and verify.
  - Figures reported by a church resource page, citing this study: 143 autistic participants; 35% said attending church was difficult because of sensory input; "almost a fifth" said the sensory environment makes them stay home. — [Autism Church resources page (secondary)](https://www.autismchurch.com/resources)
- **[PR]** A 2026 follow-on qualitative paper, "A sensory account of worship and liturgy: a qualitative study," appears in a Taylor & Francis journal (DOI prefix 1474225X). Its findings were not retrieved. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1474225X.2026.2619843)

**Autism: books on normalcy and marginalization**
- **[Book]** Armand Léon van Ommen, *Autism and Worship: A Liturgical Theology* (Baylor University Press, 2023).
  - The book argues that "normalcy" is a root cause of autistic people's absence from worship and of their being ignored there.
  - Autistic people are often overlooked or "at worst infantilized." Some are dismissed when they ask for "reasonable accommodations." They are routinely given fewer formal roles in the liturgy.
  - It includes the author's interview data and a case study of an autistic-focused congregation, the Chapel of Christ Our Hope in Singapore.
  - A Living Church reviewer notes that van Ommen is not autistic and that the book has some deficiencies, which the author partly acknowledges. — [Baylor University Press](https://www.baylorpress.com/9781481319911/autism-and-worship/); [The Living Church review](https://livingchurch.org/covenant/a-tyranny-of-normalcy/); [Reading Religion](https://readingreligion.org/9781481319898/autism-and-worship/)
- **[Book]** Grant Macaskill, *Autism and the Church: Bible, Theology, and Community* (Baylor University Press, 2019).
  - Reviewers report that he discusses how autistic people "find themselves marginalized and devalued in churches."
  - Reviewers also quote him: "The church cannot be assumed to constitute a safe and redemptive space for those with autism." — [Christian Century review](https://www.christiancentury.org/review/books/thinking-better-about-autism); [Heidelblog review](https://heidelblog.net/2021/11/review-grant-macaskill-autism-and-the-church-bible-theology-and-community-waco-tx-baylor-university-press-2019/)

**Autism: attendance data**
- **[PR]** Whitehead (2018), "Religion and Disability: Variation in Religious Service Attendance Rates for Children with Chronic Health Conditions," *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion* 57(2): 377–395, DOI 10.1111/jssr.12521.
  - Children with conditions that impede communication and social interaction are the most likely never to attend worship, and the pattern is stable over time.
  - A secondary source reports that autistic children were 1.84 times more likely never to attend. This is a child sample, but it describes the congregational histories many autistic adults grew up with. — [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jssr.12521); [Key Ministry, secondary, reports 1.84](https://keyministry.org/2018/07/22/2018-7-22-its-the-hidden-disabilities-that-keep-kids-out-of-church/)
- **[Event listing]** A University of Cambridge Faraday Institute event (November 2025) states that autistic adults and families with autistic children are "much less likely to come to church services." — [Faraday Institute](https://www.faraday.cam.ac.uk/event/neurodiversity-and-the-church/)

**ADHD**
- **[PR]** Upenieks & Fishman, "Paths of Faith and Focus? ADHD, Adult Religiosity, and Moderation by Race in the Add Health Study," *Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion*, DOI 10.1111/jssr.70089. Dates conflict between listings (2025 and 2026).
  - With N = 9,785, an ADHD diagnosis before age 18 was "strongly and persistently associated with lower religious attendance in early midlife." This held after adjusting for adolescent religiosity, demographics and health selection.
  - Associations with private religiosity were largely explained by background factors.
  - PsyPost reports about 50% lower odds of regular attendance.
  - Search summaries say the association was stronger for Black and Asian American adults than for White adults. This could not be verified from the abstract. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jssr.70089); [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/childhood-adhd-diagnosis-linked-to-lower-adult-religious-attendance/)
- **[PR]** Dew, Kollins & Koenig (2022), "ADHD, Religiosity, and Psychiatric Comorbidity in Adolescence and Adulthood," *Journal of Attention Disorders*, DOI 10.1177/1087054720972803. The study used more than 8,000 Add Health participants aged 12–34 across four waves.
  - ADHD symptoms correlated with lower levels of all religious variables at nearly all waves.
  - At Wave IV (adulthood), prayer and attendance interacted with ADHD symptoms to predict *worse* psychopathology. — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054720972803); PubMed metadata (DOI above)
- **[PR, citation only]** "Disability and Sin: A Qualitative Study of the Perspectives of Pastors," *Journal of Pastoral Theology*, surfaced only as a reference; its content was not retrieved. — referenced in [Mississippi State thesis](https://scholarsjunction.msstate.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=5612&context=td)
- **[PR, not religion-specific]** A qualitative study of adults with ADHD found that criticism from parents and teachers had "consequential effects on their self-esteem." This is relevant background for shame narratives that congregations may amplify. — [Experiences of criticism in adults with ADHD (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8856522/)

**"Spiritual deficiency" framing (practitioner sources only)**
- **[Practitioner/Opinion]** A counsellor who specializes in deconstruction says late-diagnosed autistic adults often had autistic traits framed as spiritual failings, and sensory overwhelm or meltdowns treated as lapses in faith. — [Michelle F. Moseley Counseling guest post](https://michellefmoseley.com/guest-post-religious-trauma-in-late-diagnosed-autistic-adults/)
- **[Book review]** A review of Claire Williams' *Peculiar Discipleship* notes her account of a church failing to recognize an autistic child's behaviour and spiritualizing it instead. — [Disability and Church review](https://disabilityandchurch.com/2023/11/24/peculiar-discipleship-review/)

### Inferences
- Across the qualitative studies, the barrier is less the liturgy itself than the congregation's attitudes ("normalcy," ableism, leaders who dismiss requests). This suggests attitudinal change matters as much as physical accommodations.
- The ADHD attendance gap (about half the odds of regular attendance) is large, but its causes are unknown. Executive-function friction with scheduled attendance, experiences of exclusion, and religion-specific shame are all plausible, but none has been tested.
- The Dew et al. Wave IV interaction suggests religious participation may not be uniformly protective for ADHD adults and might sometimes compound distress. The authors did not establish a mechanism.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed qualitative study was found of ADHD adults' *own* congregational experiences, such as being labelled lazy, rebellious or undisciplined in prayer or study. Nor was one found specifically on AuDHD adults.
- The labels "rebellious, lazy, spiritually deficient" are documented only in practitioner writing, not empirical studies.
- Nearly all the data are Christian, UK or US, and Protestant or Anglican. Catholic, Orthodox, Black church, Latter-day Saint and non-Western contexts are thinly covered. One RSIS paper on autism inclusion in African churches exists, but it is low-tier: [RSIS](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/view/neurodiversity-worship-and-belonging-reimagining-autism-inclusion-in-african-churches).

## 2. Religious trauma syndrome (Winell), religious scrupulosity, and their intersections with autism (literalism, rule-following, justice sensitivity) and ADHD (rejection sensitivity, shame, impulsivity framed as sin)

### Takeaway
Religious Trauma Syndrome (RTS) is a clinical concept, not a DSM diagnosis. Scrupulosity is well-established OCD phenomenology. The specific links to autism and ADHD are, as of 2026, **hypothesized in practitioner writing but not tested empirically**. No peer-reviewed study was found that measures religious trauma or spiritual abuse in autistic or ADHD adults.

### Cited Findings
**Religious Trauma Syndrome**
- **[Concept/Practitioner]** Marlene Winell coined "Religious Trauma Syndrome" in 2011 to describe symptoms that follow leaving authoritarian or controlling religion. It is not in the DSM. — [Wikipedia: Religious trauma syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_trauma_syndrome); [Winell, "Religious Trauma Syndrome: It's Time to Recognise It," *CBT Today* (PDF via Journey Free)](https://journeyfree.org/wp-content/uploads/RTS-article-in-CBT-Today.pdf); [Encyclopedia.pub entry](https://encyclopedia.pub/entry/35706)
- **[Book]** Winell's earlier book, *Leaving the Fold: A Guide for Former Fundamentalists and Others Leaving Their Religion* (New Harbinger, 1993), is a foundational self-help text. The publication details come from general knowledge and were not verified in this session's searches. — [Journey Free (Winell's organization)](https://journeyfree.org/wp-content/uploads/RTS-article-in-CBT-Today.pdf)

**Prevalence and measurement of religious trauma (not neurodivergence-specific)**
- **[Survey/PR, niche journal]** Slade et al. (2023), "Percentage of U.S. Adults Suffering from Religious Trauma: A Sociological Study," *Socio-Historical Examination of Religion and Ministry* 5(1): 1–28. With N = 1,581 US adults, it estimates that 27–33% have experienced religious trauma in their lifetime and 10–15% are currently symptomatic. No neurodivergence breakdown was found. — [Internet Archive PDF](https://ia801603.us.archive.org/3/items/sherm.2023.vol5.no1.01/Percentage%20of%20U.S.%20Adults%20Suffering%20from%20Religious%20Trauma.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369327217_Percentage_of_US_Adults_Suffering_from_Religious_Trauma_A_Sociological_Study)
- **[PR-review]** A systematic review of the empirical literature on religious and spiritual abuse and trauma identified 25 eligible studies. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362782831_Religiousspiritual_abuse_and_trauma_A_systematic_review_of_the_empirical_literature)
- **[PR-review]** A 2026 *Pastoral Psychology* paper offers an integrative clinical framework for religious and spiritual abuse, trauma and attachment. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11089-026-01375-y)
- **[Dissertation]** Koch (2025) validated a Spiritual Harm & Abuse Scale with 3,222 participants, apparently not autism-targeted. — [Northwest University archive](https://archives.northwestu.edu/bitstream/handle/nu/92491/Koch_Daniel_PsyD_2025.pdf?sequence=1&isAllowed=y)

**Trauma in autism (not religion-specific)**
- **[PR]** Autistic adults report higher trauma exposure than non-autistic adults, and research on trauma in autism is still "emerging." — [Traumatic Experiences, Psychological Distress and Suicide-Related Behaviors in Autistic Adults (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12853244/); [Trauma and Social Adversity in Autism (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11997697/)

**Scrupulosity**
- **[PR, case report]** Scrupulosity is OCD with religious or moral content, in which the feared consequence is religious or moral. — [Starvation in OCD due to scrupulosity (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2915602/)

**Hypothesized autism and ADHD intersections (practitioner sources only)**
- **[Practitioner/Opinion]** Clinicians argue that "many autistic brains thrive on binary categorization, and religious dogma satisfies this cognitive preference." They also argue that the neurodivergent brain "often cannot let contradictions go," and that high-control religion can cause lasting injury. — [Resilient Mind Counseling](https://resilientmindcounseling.com/religious-trauma-neurodivergence-autism-adhd/); [Divergent Path Wellness on scrupulosity, OCD and religious trauma](https://www.divergentpathwellness.com/post/scrupulosity-ocd-and-religious-trauma)
- **[Practitioner/Opinion]** An autistic former evangelical writer argues that a faith offering one "correct way" to live can relieve autistic decision fatigue at first, then tie self-worth to rule-keeping. — [Cat Salladin, "Charismatic evangelicalism and neurodivergence" (Substack)](https://catsalladin.substack.com/p/charismatic-evangelicalism-and-neurodivergence)
- **[Practitioner/Opinion]** An ADHD-and-faith blog argues that demonic or sin framings of ADHD keep people from effective treatment and deepen shame. — [NeuroLaunch](https://neurolaunch.com/adhd-and-christianity/)

**Book on autistic trauma and church**
- **[Book]** Claire Williams, *Peculiar Discipleship: An Autistic Liberation Theology* (SCM Press, 2023). Its chapters include trauma and ecclesiology, written from the author's experience as an autistic Christian with a progressive Pentecostal background. — [SCM Press](https://scmpress.hymnsam.co.uk/books/9780334063063/peculiar-discipleship); [Review by Emma Percy, *Theology* (2024)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0040571X231222201o)

### Inferences
- These mechanisms are plausible: autistic literalism and rule-adherence absorbing doctrine and threat messages fully, justice sensitivity clashing with hypocrisy, and ADHD rejection sensitivity and impulsivity being moralized as sin. The report should present them as **hypotheses from clinical observation**, not established findings.
- Autistic adults' higher baseline trauma exposure and the roughly 27–33% US lifetime religious-trauma estimate together suggest neurodivergent people in high-control settings may carry compounded risk. No dataset tests this.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found measuring religious trauma, RTS symptoms or spiritual abuse specifically in autistic, ADHD or AuDHD adults. Searches covered PubMed and the web for "religious trauma," "spiritual abuse," "autis*" and "ADHD."
- No study was found on scrupulosity rates in autistic or ADHD adults, though OCD comorbidity with autism is well documented elsewhere and outside this search.
- No research was found linking rejection sensitive dysphoria (itself not a formal diagnosis) to religious experience.

## 3. Studies or surveys on neurodivergent people and deconstruction, exvangelical movements, purity culture, or deliverance/exorcism framings of autism and ADHD

### Takeaway
There are **no located surveys or empirical studies** on neurodivergent exvangelicals or deconstruction. Purity culture and autism appear only in commentary. Deliverance framings of autism and ADHD are documented in scholarly commentary, in parents' accounts within qualitative research, and in ministry materials that openly promote "deliverance from autism or ADHD." Survivor data are anecdotal.

### Cited Findings
**Purity culture**
- **[Commentary in a journal]** "Autism and Purity Culture," *Canadian Journal of Theology, Mental Health and Disability* (2022). It is a first-person and document-based exploration of autistic people's vulnerability to purity culture's messages about suppressing self and sexuality, not survey research. — [CJTMHD](https://jps.library.utoronto.ca/index.php/cjtmhd/article/view/39544)
- **[Opinion]** An advocacy essay argues that autistic girls in purity culture may be punished for expressive dress or for taking words literally. — [Gutsy Christianity, "Purity Culture Endangers Autistic Girls" (Substack)](https://gutsychristianity.substack.com/p/purity-culture-endangers-autistic)

**Deliverance and exorcism**
- **[PR]** Mercer (2013), "Deliverance, demonic possession, and mental illness: some considerations for mental health professionals," *Mental Health, Religion & Culture* 16: 595–611. Deliverance practitioners claim to treat schizophrenia, **ADHD** and Reactive Attachment Disorder, which they attribute to the person's own or an ancestor's sins. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254320234_Deliverance_demonic_possession_and_mental_illness_some_considerations_for_mental_health_professionals)
- **[PR]** "The Bible, Autism and Other Profound Developmental Conditions: Regulating Hermeneutics," *Journal of Disability & Religion* (2021). In research the paper draws on, parents in many cases described praying, unsuccessfully, for their autistic children to be delivered from demons. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23312521.2021.1881024)
- **[Ministry commentary]** A church-inclusion ministry reports a pastor insisting autistic children are demon-possessed. It references an *Indianapolis Monthly* story of an attempted exorcism of an autistic teen and a reported death of an autistic 8-year-old in Milwaukee during an attempted exorcism. These cases were not independently verified here. — [Church4EveryChild (Key Ministry)](https://church4everychild.org/2014/07/24/when-church-leaders-talk-about-mental-illness-spiritual-evil-and-demons/)
- **[Primary-source examples of the framing]** Ministry content promotes "deliverance prayer from autism" and testimonies of "deliverance" from ADHD as a "demonic curse." — [Becky Dvorak, "Deliverance Prayer From Autism"](https://authorbeckydvorak.com/deliverance-prayer-from-austism/); [Royal Girlz, "The Gates of Autism and Deliverance"](https://royalgirlz.com/the-gates-of-autism/); [Medium testimony on ADHD as a demonic curse](https://medium.com/@bchaydel/god-delivered-me-from-20-demonic-curses-in-a-year-the-greatest-being-adhd-651f0523c9d8)
- **[Thesis]** A UTSA thesis titled "Pray the ADHD Away? An Analysis of the Intergenerational…" exists, but its content was not retrieved. — [UTSA repository](https://rrpress.utsa.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/dd14610b-eb45-4509-8013-b8c703caf26b/content)

**Deconstruction and late diagnosis**
- **[Practitioner/Opinion]** "For many late-diagnosed autistic adults, the journey of deconstruction isn't just about theology. It is about identity." — [Michelle F. Moseley Counseling](https://michellefmoseley.com/guest-post-religious-trauma-in-late-diagnosed-autistic-adults/)
- **[Podcast]** SquarePeg S7 Ep2, "God as a special interest: deconstructing religious identity as a newly identified autistic." The host, diagnosed at 37, describes deconstructing her religious identity alongside her emerging autistic identity. — [SquarePeg](https://squarepeg.community/82-s7-ep2-god-as-a-special-interest-deconstructing-religious-identity-as-a-newly-identified-autistic/)
- **[PR, adjacent]** Late-diagnosis research describes diagnosis as a "turning point" for reinterpreting one's life history, followed by a period of identity reconstruction. This is a plausible frame for faith re-evaluation, though these studies do not address religion. — [MDPI *J Clin Med* 2025, Experiences of an Autism Diagnosis in Adulthood](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/14/12/4315); [From diagnosis to dialogue: older adults with late autism diagnosis (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12526855/)
- **[Reference]** Faith deconstruction is the process of re-examining and questioning religious beliefs. Reconstruction, meaning retaining or rebuilding belief, is one possible outcome. — [Wikipedia: Faith deconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith_deconstruction)

### Inferences
- The late-diagnosis "turning point" literature plus practitioner reports point to a common pattern. After diagnosis, people re-read earlier spiritual "failures" (inattention in prayer, meltdowns, social non-conformity) as neurotype rather than sin. This can drive either deconstruction or a reconstructed, affirming faith. The pattern is a reasonable synthesis, but it has not been empirically tested.
- Deliverance framings are best documented in Pentecostal and charismatic contexts and around children. Adults who grew up in those settings may carry related harm, but no adult survivor data were found.

### Gaps
- No survey or study was found on neurodivergent people within exvangelical or deconstruction communities. No breakdown of the Slade et al. religious-trauma survey by neurotype was found.
- No adult survivor research on deliverance or exorcism experiences related to autism or ADHD was found.
- The prevalence of deliverance framing across denominations or traditions is unknown.

## 4. What faith communities offer that helps (structure, ritual, belonging, liturgy), according to research

### Takeaway
Evidence that religion helps autistic adults is modest and mostly qualitative. Predictable liturgy, sensory and social control, real belonging once needs are met, and freedom to build a personal belief system all emerge as helpful. Research on cognition and religiosity in autism is inconsistent, so "autistic people are less religious" should not be stated as fact.

### Cited Findings
**Qualitative evidence**
- **[PR]** Autistic interviewees described liturgy as "predictable and easy to learn and memorize," a subtheme within "the experience of liturgy." — [van Ommen & Endress 2022, *Studia Liturgica*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/00393207221111573)
- **[PR]** Autistic participants felt more connected to God when they controlled the sensory and social aspects of church, and they enjoyed some sensory aspects of worship as much as non-autistic participants did. — [Unwin & van Ommen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/00916471241266810)
- **[PR]** Belonging becomes possible "when needs were met and autistic embodiment was recognized and valued." — [Waldock 2023, JDR](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/23312521.2023.2249452)
- **[Book]** Van Ommen proposes a "liturgical theology of availability," built on kenosis and participation, in response to normalcy. — [Baylor University Press](https://www.baylorpress.com/9781481319911/autism-and-worship/); [*Identity, (Dis)Ability and Belonging* paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/387403160_Identity_DisAbility_and_Belonging_Liturgy_and_Worship_through_the_Lens_of_Autism)
- **[PR]** Julian of Norwich has been read as a model of "autistic expressions of Christian spirituality" (Project MUSE). The details were not retrieved. — [Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/article/972515)
- **[Journalism]** A Mercer University initiative funded by the Lilly Endowment argues that order-focused worship can make inclusion harder. It favours "a gracious, openhanded set of worship practices that emphasize flexibility and freedom." This is in tension with the finding that predictable liturgy helps; the likely resolution is predictable structure combined with freedom of bodily and sensory participation. — [Christianity Today, July 2025](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2025/07/autism-worship-church-neurodivergence-inclusion-research/)

**Cognition and belief: a contested picture**
- **[PR]** Caldwell-Harris et al. (2011), Cognitive Science Society proceedings. The study analysed forum content from 192 posters on WrongPlanet and surveyed 61 adults with "high-functioning autism." Autistic participants were "much more likely … to identify as atheist or agnostic, and, if religious, … to construct their own religious belief system." A Daily Mail report, quoted secondhand, gave 26% atheist vs 16% of neurotypicals; this was not verified against the paper. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Religious-Belief-Systems-of-Persons-with-High-Caldwell-Harris-Murphy/eee1b8dc12d219020b2ec500abdebc442f51ebc7); [PDF mirror](https://autismodiario.com/wp-content/uploads/2011/09/Religious-Belief-Systems-of-Persons-with-High-Functioning-Autism-.pdf)
- **[PR]** Norenzayan, Gervais & Trzesniewski (2012), "Mentalizing Deficits Constrain Belief in a Personal God," *PLoS ONE*. Autistic adolescents reported less belief in God than controls, and mentalizing, not attendance, explained the link. — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0036880)
- **[PR]** Crespi et al. (2019), *PLoS ONE*, studied undergraduates using the Autism Quotient (AQ).
  - Total spirituality correlated negatively with AQ, driven by the "Search for Meaning" subscale.
  - Belief in God was **uncorrelated** with autism traits. — [PLoS ONE, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0213456](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0213456) (PubMed abstract)
- **[PR-review]** Burnos & Kopacz (2025), "Religiosity of adults on the autism spectrum: a cognitive and empirical analysis," *Frontiers in Psychiatry*, DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692.
  - The review covers nine empirical studies and finds the picture "complex and marked by inconsistency."
  - The mentalizing-reduces-religiosity hypothesis "has not been unequivocally supported." Results on overall religiosity and God-representations are "inconclusive."
  - It lists barriers and needs linked to predictability needs, cognitive rigidity, restricted interests and executive function. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692/full); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/393412347_Religiosity_of_adults_on_the_autism_spectrum_a_cognitive_and_empirical_analysis)
- **[PR]** Visuri and Clark (2019), "Autism and the panoply of religious belief, disbelief and experience," argues autistic religiosity is diverse rather than simply reduced. — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/15220847/Clark_K_J_and_Visuri_I_2019_Autism_and_the_panoply_of_religious_belief_disbelief_and_experience)
- **[PR]** *Religions* (MDPI) 2025, 16(3): 371, "Autism Spectrum Disorders, Anxiety, and Religion: The Role of Personality Traits," discusses religion and spirituality as both supporting and hindering coping. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/3/371)
- **[Reference work]** Springer encyclopedia entry, "Autistic People, Religion, and Spirituality." — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-032-11375-7_400)

### Inferences
- What helps appears to be **predictability plus agency**: fixed liturgical forms, combined with control over sensory input, seating, participation and social contact.
- The "construct their own belief system" finding (Caldwell-Harris) fits reports of late-diagnosed adults reconstructing idiosyncratic or contemplative faith rather than either staying unchanged or leaving entirely.

### Gaps
- No outcome studies were found (for example, on wellbeing or mental health) of autistic or ADHD adults in affirming versus non-affirming congregations.
- No research was found on what helps ADHD adults specifically, such as shorter services, movement or flexible attendance.
- One search summary mentioned a 2025 survey of more than 2,000 people (38% autistic or with autistic traits) reporting *higher* religious practice and more personal belief systems among autistic respondents. The primary source could not be identified, so it should not be cited without verification.

## 5. Neurodivergent-affirming congregational efforts and resources (Christian, Jewish, Muslim and other)

### Takeaway
Affirming resources are concentrated in UK academic theology (Aberdeen's Centre for Autism and Theology), autistic-authored theology (Williams, Waldock), US disability-ministry organizations, and Jewish inclusion initiatives. Muslim resources are mostly family- and child-focused. Media coverage in 2025–2026 highlights sensory rooms and flexible worship, with caveats that rooms alone don't create belonging.

### Cited Findings
**Christian academic and autistic-authored work**
- **[Academic centre]** The Centre for Autism and Theology at the University of Aberdeen is co-directed by Armand Léon van Ommen. It publishes resources and an e-zine, "Re-Framing Church through the Lens of Autism." — [CAT resources](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/dhpa/research/centres/centre-for-autism-and-theology/resources/); [Re-Framing Church e-zine](https://abdn.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/re-framing-church-through-the-lens-of-autism/); [van Ommen profile](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/people/leon.vanommen)
- **[Book]** Grant Macaskill, *Autism and the Church* (2019). He frames autism as difference rather than deficiency and treats neurodiversity and disability as not mutually exclusive. He recommends accommodations such as space to decompress from social interaction. Reviewers call it a "scoping work." — [Baylor University Press](https://www.baylorpress.com/9781481311250/autism-and-the-church/); [Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/review/books/thinking-better-about-autism); [Reformed Faith & Practice](https://journal.rts.edu/review/autism-and-the-church-bible-theology-and-community/); [Themelios](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/review/autism-and-the-church/)
- **[Book]** Claire Williams, *Peculiar Discipleship: An Autistic Liberation Theology* (SCM Press, 2023; 272 pp.). Williams is a teacher and researcher at Regents Theological College. The book is described as theology *from* neurodiversity, calling for "radical hospitality" made up of attention, access, acceptance and imagination. — [SCM Press](https://scmpress.hymnsam.co.uk/books/9780334063063/peculiar-discipleship); [*Theology* review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0040571X231222201o)
- **[Book]** Armand Léon van Ommen, *Autism and Worship* (2023), as above. — [Baylor University Press](https://www.baylorpress.com/9781481319911/autism-and-worship/)
- **[Resource]** Autism Church resources page, which compiles research summaries. — [autismchurch.com](https://www.autismchurch.com/resources)
- **[Resource]** NeurodiverseChurch.org, including a review of *Autism and Worship*. — [Neurodiverse Church](https://www.neurodiversechurch.org/misc/book-review-autism-and-worship-by-armand-leon-van-ommen/)
- **[Ministry]** Key Ministry, a US disability-inclusion ministry focused on hidden disabilities, which also runs church4everychild. — [Key Ministry](https://keyministry.org/2018/07/22/2018-7-22-its-the-hidden-disabilities-that-keep-kids-out-of-church/)

**Journalism on congregational practice (2025–2026)**
- **[Journalism]** RNS, syndicated by U.S. News (July 2025), reports on sensory rooms in temples and churches in New Jersey. For people who struggle to process sensory input, "houses of worship can quickly feel anything but holy." A Latter-day Saint disability specialist cautions that a sensory room alone does not make a congregation accessible. — [U.S. News / RNS](https://www.usnews.com/news/best-states/new-jersey/articles/2025-07-22/in-temples-and-churches-sensory-rooms-remove-barriers-for-neurodivergent-worshipers)
- **[Journalism]** *Christianity Today* (July 2025), "Inclusive Worship Shouts, Shushes, and Sings to the Lord," covers scholarship on neurodivergent worshippers, a dissertation on autism inclusion in Southern Baptist worship, and the Mercer/Lilly initiative. — [Christianity Today](https://www.christianitytoday.com/2025/07/autism-worship-church-neurodivergence-inclusion-research/)
- **[Denominational news]** PC(USA), August 2025: "Helping worshipers who are neurodivergent feel right at home." — [PCUSA](https://pcusa.org/news-storytelling/news/2025/8/27/helping-worshipers-who-are-neurodivergent-feel-right-home)
- **[Journalism]** MinistryWatch describes quiet spaces, buddy systems, and inviting autistic members to share their stories from the pulpit. — [MinistryWatch](https://ministrywatch.com/as-autism-diagnoses-rise-christian-ministries-adapt/)
- **[Blog]** A Kansas City blog report (2026) describes slower, contemplative Lenten services with prayer stations and headphones. — [Discipleship Commons](https://discipleshipcommons.blog/2026/03/15/midwest-churches-expand-efforts-to-welcome-people-with-autism/)

**Jewish**
- **[PR]** Sher, Gibson & Sher (2022), *Autism*, an interpretative phenomenological analysis of autism stigma in the UK Jewish community ("We've come a very, very, long way"). Reports of rabbis and congregations accepting autistic children even when they disrupted services served as potent counter-stigma examples. The focus is on families and children. — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13623613221075099)
- **[Survey]** Disability Belongs' 2021 Faith Inclusion Survey, Jewish responses, includes exclusion accounts such as a son asked to leave a synagogue junior choir as "disruptive." — [Disability Belongs](https://www.disabilitybelongs.org/faith-spirituality/2021-faith-survey-jewish/)
- **[Journalism]** JTA (2012) reports synagogues slowly expanding inclusion to people with emotional and mental disabilities. Attitudinal barriers are key: congregants acknowledge physical disability more readily than cognitive disability. — [JTA](https://www.jta.org/2012/04/30/lifestyle/synagogues-slowly-expanding-inclusion-to-those-with-emotional-mental-disabilities)
- **[Opinion]** Jewish Women's Archive, Rising Voices blog: "Embracing Neurodivergence in the Jewish Community." — [JWA](https://jwa.org/blog/risingvoices/embracing-neurodivergence-jewish-community)
- The Ruderman Synagogue Inclusion Project (Boston area, 2015–2023) is reported to have worked with more than 70 synagogues on accessibility. This came from a search summary only, so verify at Ruderman Family Foundation sources.

**Muslim**
- **[PR]** Jegatheesan et al., a study of South Asian Muslim immigrant families (3 families). Parents saw full inclusion of their autistic children as depending on mosque participation. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245925148_Autism_From_a_Religious_Perspective_A_Study_of_Parental_Beliefs_in_South_Asian_Muslim_Immigrant_Families)
- **[PR]** An Indonesian study of Muslim parents' perceptions of autism (5 parents) found that faith helped them accept the diagnosis. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/305072743_How_Religious_Beliefs_Influence_Understanding_on_Disability_A_Study_of_Muslim_Family's_Perception_on_Autism)
- **[Book chapter]** Laila Dahan, "The Heart of Autism: Building a Positive Islamic Model of Cognitive Disability" (Springer, 2021). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-72606-5_13)
- **[Organization]** The American Muslim Women's Research and Resource Institute (AMWRRI) has a "Muslims with Disabilities" page. Search summaries also describe MUHSEN's mosque inclusion work (such as no-wait Ramadan iftar lines for special-needs families) and a Muslim Social Research Network needs study spanning the US, UK and Canada. — [AMWRRI](https://amwrri.org/muslims-with-disabilities/)

### Inferences
- Affirming resources have shifted from "special needs ministry for children" toward adult, autistic-authored theology since about 2019. This literature is still mostly Christian and UK-centred.
- Journalism repeatedly notes that physical accommodations (sensory rooms, headphones) are necessary but insufficient, which echoes the academic finding that normalcy and attitudes are the deeper barrier.

### Gaps
- **Ruth Gilbert:** no relevant autism-and-faith work by this name was located. The name may be misattributed, so the coordinator should verify.
- **Bethany Fellowship and accessibility ministries:** no neurodivergence-specific resources under this name were found. Bethesda, Joni and Friends, and Disability Belongs (formerly RespectAbility's faith work, per site branding) would be logical checks, but they were not researched in depth.
- No adult-focused peer-reviewed study of autistic or ADHD Muslims, Jews, Hindus, Sikhs or Buddhists in their own words was found. Non-Christian evidence is almost entirely about families and children.
- The Ruderman and MUHSEN details were not verified at primary sources.

## 6. Data on rates of religious disaffiliation among neurodivergent adults

### Takeaway
**No direct data on disaffiliation** (leaving a religion or "nones" status) by neurotype were found. The best proxies are lower adult attendance after childhood ADHD diagnosis (about half the odds) and higher atheist or agnostic identification in small autistic samples (2011–2012). Later reviews call the autism findings inconsistent.

### Cited Findings
- **[PR]** ADHD diagnosed before 18 is associated with about 50% lower odds of regular religious attendance in early midlife (N = 9,785, Add Health). The link with private religiosity is largely explained by background factors, which suggests disengagement from institutions rather than loss of belief. — [Upenieks & Fishman, JSSR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jssr.70089); [PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/childhood-adhd-diagnosis-linked-to-lower-adult-religious-attendance/)
- **[PR]** ADHD symptoms correlated with lower levels of all religious variables across four Add Health waves (N > 8,000, ages 12–34). — [Dew, Kollins & Koenig 2022, *J Atten Disord*](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1087054720972803)
- **[PR]** Autistic adults (N = 61 surveyed, plus 192 forum posters) were more likely to identify as atheist or agnostic. A secondhand figure gives 26% vs 16% atheist. — [Caldwell-Harris et al. 2011](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Religious-Belief-Systems-of-Persons-with-High-Caldwell-Harris-Murphy/eee1b8dc12d219020b2ec500abdebc442f51ebc7)
- **[PR]** Autistic adolescents reported less belief in God than controls. — [Norenzayan et al. 2012, *PLoS ONE*](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0036880)
- **[PR-review]** In contrast, a 2025 review of nine studies calls overall religiosity findings "inconclusive," and some studies fail to find the autism–atheism link. — [Burnos & Kopacz 2025, *Frontiers in Psychiatry*](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1594692/full)
- **[PR]** Autistic children are more likely never to attend worship (1.84 times, per a secondary source). — [Whitehead 2018, JSSR](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jssr.12521); [Key Ministry](https://keyministry.org/2018/07/22/2018-7-22-its-the-hidden-disabilities-that-keep-kids-out-of-church/)
- **[PR]** Almost a fifth of autistic respondents in one 2024 survey said the sensory environment keeps them home from church. — [Unwin & van Ommen 2024](https://doi.org/10.1177/00916471241266810), via [Autism Church](https://www.autismchurch.com/resources)

### Inferences
- The pattern across sources is that **non-attendance and institutional disengagement** are well evidenced for ADHD and plausible for autism, while **loss of belief** is less clearly documented. The report should distinguish "leaving church" from "leaving faith."
- Lower attendance may reflect exclusion and access barriers rather than personal choice. The sensory "stay home" data support this for autism.

### Gaps
- No Pew, PRRI, Barna, Gallup or other national survey was found that breaks down disaffiliation or "nones" by autism or ADHD status.
- No data were found on disaffiliation rates after late diagnosis specifically, nor any AuDHD-specific figures.
- The race-moderation results in Upenieks & Fishman (reported as a stronger association for Black and Asian American adults) need verification from the full text.
