# Late Diagnosis (Adult/Midlife) of Autism, ADHD and AuDHD as an Identity, Meaning-Making and Existential/Spiritual Turning Point

> Notes on method and sources. Most bibliographic data and abstract content below were retrieved through PubMed (abstract-level reading; DOIs given). Full-text fetches from journals.sagepub.com and www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov were blocked by this session's network settings. Some findings therefore rest on abstracts, plus web-search snippets where noted. Labels: **[PR]** = peer-reviewed, **[PR-review]** = peer-reviewed review or clinical guidance, **[POP]** = popular, clinician or blog source (not peer-reviewed). Where the user's brief named a study, I checked the citation. Corrections are flagged (e.g., Young et al. is dated 2007 in PubMed, not 2008).

## Q1. What do studies on late AUTISM diagnosis find about identity reconstruction, grief, relief and reinterpretation of the past?

### Takeaway
Across UK and Australian qualitative studies (mostly women, with ages at diagnosis from the late 30s to over 50), late autism diagnosis is mostly experienced as an explanatory, positive "reconfiguration of self." It replaces self-blame with self-understanding and self-compassion and prompts a re-reading of the whole life story ("re-living life through a new lens"). The relief comes alongside grief, pain and adjustment difficulty, which are sharpest because the diagnosis comes so late. Identity change is described as staged, non-linear, and heavily mediated by autistic community and peer support.

### Cited Findings

**Leedham et al. (women diagnosed over 40)** [PR]
- Leedham A, Thompson AR, Smith R, Freeth M. "'I was exhausted trying to figure it out': The experiences of females receiving an autism diagnosis in middle to late adulthood." *Autism* 2020;24(1):135–146 (online May 2019). Method: 11 autistic females diagnosed **over age 40**, semi-structured interviews, Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA). — [DOI 10.1177/1362361319853442](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319853442)
- Superordinate themes: pre-diagnosis "pretending to be normal and fitting in" and "mental health and mislabelling"; post-diagnosis "initial reactions and search for understanding" and "**re-living life through a new lens**"; others' "stereotyped assumptions"; and "negotiating relationships, connections and community", "changing well-being and views of the self", and "the meaning of diagnosis." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319853442)
- Key finding: diagnosis was experienced "as facilitating **transition from being self-critical to self-compassionate**, coupled with an increased sense of agency." Participants experienced "a change in identity that enabled greater acceptance and understanding of the self. However, this was **painful to adjust to at such a late stage**." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319853442)

**Stagg & Belcher (adults over 50)** [PR]
- Stagg SD, Belcher H. "Living with autism without knowing: receiving a diagnosis in later life." *Health Psychology and Behavioral Medicine* 2019;7(1):348–361. Described as "the first study to investigate this population." Nine adults **over age 50**, recently diagnosed, thematic analysis. — [DOI 10.1080/21642850.2019.1684920](https://doi.org/10.1080/21642850.2019.1684920)
- Participants had been treated for anxiety and depression. Growing up they "felt **isolated and alien**." Diagnosis "was seen as a positive step and allowed for a **reconfiguration of self** and an appreciation of individual needs." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/21642850.2019.1684920)
- Related work from the same group: probably-autistic undiagnosed women (AQ ≥32) were more likely than diagnosed autistic women to have received a Borderline Personality Disorder diagnosis. Autistic women had typically received more mental-health diagnoses before their autism diagnosis than autistic men, which documents the "mislabelling" history behind late diagnosis (Belcher, Morein-Zamir, Stagg, Ford, *J Autism Dev Disord* 2023;53(8):3118–3132). — [DOI 10.1007/s10803-022-05583-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-022-05583-2)

**Punshon et al. (the "not guilty verdict")** [PR]
- Punshon C, Skirrow P, Murphy G. "The not guilty verdict: psychological reactions to a diagnosis of Asperger syndrome in adulthood." *Autism* 2009;13(3):265–283. Phenomenological study, 10 adults (male and female; MeSH includes "Middle Aged"). — [DOI 10.1177/1362361309103795](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361309103795)
- Six themes: negative life experiences and pre-diagnosis services led to beliefs about "symptoms" that shaped perceived self-identity. How people felt on receiving the diagnosis linked to their current beliefs about themselves, and societal views of Asperger syndrome mattered. The authors frame the findings within identity-formation literature. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361309103795)
- The title metaphor ("not guilty verdict") frames diagnosis as **exoneration/absolution** from a lifetime of implied blame. This is a quasi-moral or juridical framing, not a spiritual one (see Q4). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361309103795)

**Lilley et al. (oral histories, diagnosed after 35, born before 1975)** [PR]
- Lilley R, Lawson W, Hall G, Mahony J, Clapham H, Heyworth M, Arnold SRC, Trollor JN, Yudell M, Pellicano E. "'A way to be me': Autobiographical reflections of autistic adults diagnosed in mid-to-late adulthood." *Autism* 2022;26(6):1395–1408 (online Oct 2021). 26 Australian autistic adults, all **born before 1975** and formally identified **after age 35**. Oral history method; autistic interviewers; participatory analysis. — [DOI 10.1177/13623613211050694](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613211050694)
- Participants described "many negative experiences, including **trauma**, which had shaped how they think about themselves." For most, diagnosis "had a very positive impact on their sense of self, allowing them to **understand more about their own past** and to feel good about their autistic identity." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613211050694)
- The authors explicitly challenge older claims that autistic people have an "impaired sense of self." They show deep autobiographical reflection on a "changing sense of self-identity over time." Keywords include masking, stigma and trauma. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613211050694)
- A companion paper from the same oral-history project, "'Peas in a pod': Oral History Reflections on Autistic Identity in Family and Community by Late-Diagnosed Adults," reports that autistic community supports the "active creation of shared Autistic identity" (seen via search snippet only; full text not fetched). — [PMC9986211](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9986211/)

**Arnold et al. (measuring impact of diagnosis)** [PR]
- Arnold SRC, Huang Y, Hwang YI, Richdale AL, Trollor JN, Lawson LP. "'The Single Most Important Thing That Has Happened to Me in My Life': Development of the Impact of Diagnosis Scale–Preliminary Revision." *Autism in Adulthood* 2020;2(1):34–41. N=92 autistic adolescents and adults (Australian SASLA and ALSAA cohorts). Factors: "Service Access," "Being Understood," "Self-Acceptance and Understanding." Impact was generally positive for Self-Acceptance/Understanding but **neutral** for the other domains. — [DOI 10.1089/aut.2019.0059](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2019.0059)
- Arnold SRC et al. "Development of the Impact of Diagnosis Scale–Revised (IODS-R)." *Assessment* 2023;31(4):908–919. Co-produced with autistic advisors from a "non-deficit perspective": 46 items reduced to 22 across 4 domains, N=125 formally diagnosed autistic adults. Notes autism "is increasingly late diagnosed in adulthood." — [DOI 10.1177/10731911231196486](https://doi.org/10.1177/10731911231196486)
- Huang Y, Arnold SRC, Foley K-R, Trollor JN. "Diagnosis of autism in adulthood: A scoping review." *Autism* 2020;24(6):1311–1327. Adults "often have strong emotions after being diagnosed," the process is unclear and variable, and few post-diagnostic supports exist. — [DOI 10.1177/1362361320903128](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361320903128)

**Women and gender-diverse samples** [PR]
- Harmens M, Sedgewick F, Hobson H. "Autistic women's diagnostic experiences: Interactions with identity and impacts on well-being." *Women's Health* 2022;18. Mixed methods, 96 women at three stages (self-identifying/awaiting, assessment/recently diagnosed, >1 year post-diagnosis); 24 interviewed. Well-being differed across stages in health satisfaction, psychological and environmental domains. Well-being and identity changed "in a **non-linear** manner" along the diagnostic journey. — [DOI 10.1177/17455057221137477](https://doi.org/10.1177/17455057221137477)
- Harmens M, Sedgewick F, Hobson H. "The Quest for Acceptance: A Blog-Based Study of Autistic Women's Experiences and Well-Being During Autism Identification and Diagnosis." *Autism in Adulthood* 2022;4(1):42–51. 20 blogsites (23 women). Central theme: **acceptance**, both self-understanding/self-acceptance and being accepted by others, including the autism community. There was "exhaustion resulting from trying to be accepted." Post-diagnosis difficulties often came from facing **male stereotypes** of autism. — [DOI 10.1089/aut.2021.0016](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2021.0016)
- Tien I, Pearson A, Sozeri S, Seers K. "'Only Boys Can be Autistic': A Qualitative Exploration of Gender Stereotype and Socialization on the Diagnostic Journey." *Autism in Adulthood* 2025 (vol 8(4):698–709). N=24: **14 genderqueer**, 8 female, 2 male. Female socialization "directly led to the suppression of their autistic traits," and clinicians and family interpreted symptoms differently in non-males, producing late or missed diagnoses. — [DOI 10.1089/aut.2024.0178](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2024.0178)

**Staged identity frameworks** [PR]
- Bransgrove K, Karakas G. "'Being Autistic is Kind of Who You Are, It's an Identity Rather than a Disorder': Identity Negotiation and Construction Among Autistic Adults Diagnosed in Later Life." *J Autism Dev Disord* 2025 (vol 56(10):3970–3987). N=8, thematic analysis. Produces a **new staged identity framework** compared against Taylor's Cognitive Adaptation Model (1983). Social, formal, media and peer support help people "embrace their autistic identity." — [DOI 10.1007/s10803-025-06865-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-025-06865-1)

### Inferences
- The recurring structure is (1) a lifetime of feeling "alien," "different," mislabelled or blamed; (2) diagnosis as explanatory key or "verdict"; (3) **autobiographical re-reading** of the past; (4) a shift from self-criticism to self-compassion; (5) identity rebuilt through community. This resembles a narrative "life review." No study I found uses Butler's or Erikson's life-review framework explicitly.
- Grief is real but under-specified in the autism abstracts. Leedham's "painful to adjust to at such a late stage" is the most explicit statement. In autism studies, grief seems less formally modelled than in Young's ADHD stage model (Q2).

### Gaps
- I could not access full texts, so participant quotes (e.g., on grief for "lost years," or "finding my tribe") are not reproduced verbatim except where they appear in titles or abstracts.
- Few studies stratify by age at diagnosis within midlife (40s vs 50s vs 60s+). Stagg & Belcher (50+) is the main older-age study. Studies of people diagnosed in their 60s and 70s appear rare.
- Gender-diverse people are well represented in Tien et al. but mostly absent in the earlier women-only studies.

## Q2. What do studies on late ADHD diagnosis find (grief, "what might have been," women diagnosed late, menopause)?

### Takeaway
The foundational IPA study (Young et al.) proposed a six-stage acceptance model running from **relief and elation** through **anger** and **sadness/grief** to **accommodation and acceptance**. Later work on late-diagnosed women (mean age about 39; a 2026 study of women aged 45–58) repeats the themes of validation, self-compassion and reframing of strengths. It also places hormonal transitions, especially perimenopause, at the centre of symptom change and of the decision to seek diagnosis.

### Cited Findings

**Young et al. stage model** [PR]
- Young S, Bramham J, Gray K, Rose E. "The experience of receiving a diagnosis and treatment of ADHD in adulthood: a qualitative study of clinically referred patients using interpretative phenomenological analysis." *J Atten Disord* **2007**;11(4):493–503. This is the study often cited as "Young et al. 2008." PubMed lists it as 2007. N=8 adults diagnosed at a tertiary UK service; IPA. — [DOI 10.1177/1087054707305172](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054707305172)
- Master themes: (a) **review of the past**, especially feeling different from others; (b) emotional impact of the diagnosis; (c) consideration of the future. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054707305172)
- **Six-stage model of psychological acceptance**: (1) relief and elation, (2) confusion and emotional turmoil, (3) anger, (4) **sadness and grief**, (5) anxiety, (6) accommodation and acceptance. The authors recommend psychological treatment starting at the point of diagnosis. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054707305172)
- Companion study from partners' perspective: Young S, Gray K, Bramham J. *J Atten Disord* 2009 (online 2008);12(4):299–307. Eight partners. Themes: "perceptions of inadequacy of the ADHD partner," "emotional impact of diagnosis," "medication not a panacea." — [DOI 10.1177/1087054707311659](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054707311659)

**Women diagnosed late** [PR]
- Attoe DE, Climie EA. "Miss. Diagnosis: A Systematic Review of ADHD in Adult Women." *J Atten Disord* 2023;27(7):645–657. Eight studies. Themes: impacts on social-emotional wellbeing, difficult relationships, lack of control, and **self-acceptance after diagnosis**. — [DOI 10.1177/10870547231161533](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547231161533)
- Babinski DE, Libsack EJ. "Adult Diagnosis of ADHD in Women: A Mixed Methods Investigation." *J Atten Disord* 2025;29(3):207–219. 14 women (mean age ≈39.4, SD 6.4), two virtual focus groups. Benefits: "**validation/self-compassion**, adaptive coping, and social support." Costs: access difficulties, burden of care, treatment limits, stigma. Many had not recognized their own symptoms, and there was "considerable self-advocacy to convince their providers." — [DOI 10.1177/10870547241297897](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547241297897)
- Kelly T, May B, Porter …, Palace … "Exploring Women's Experiences of a Late ADHD Diagnosis: Unveiling a Personal Superpower Through Reflexive Thematic Analysis." *J Atten Disord* 2026. 12 women aged **45 to 58**. Years of misdiagnosis, internalized stigma and emotional dysregulation, "often compounded by **hormonal fluctuations** that impacted symptom severity and medication efficacy." Many reframed hyperfocus as a strength and described diagnosis as "**profoundly validating**." — [DOI 10.1177/10870547261469625](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547261469625)
- Rosenthal E, Mitchell JT, Weisner TS, Silverstein M et al. "What Can Adults With ADHD Tell Us About Their Experiences? A Review of Qualitative Methods…" *J Atten Disord* 2025;29(13):1190–1212. Scoping review of 41 qualitative studies. Studies of adults diagnosed in adulthood "often examine **emotional responses to receiving the diagnosis**." Identity and stigma concerns recur across subgroups. — [DOI 10.1177/10870547251352589](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547251352589)
- Krishnamoorthy T, Das S, Thomas N. "Stigma in adults with ADHD: a systematic review…" *Front Psychiatry* 2026;17:1783271. 17 papers mapped onto WHO QoL domains, including the "**spirituality/religion/personal beliefs**" domain. The abstract does not report spirituality-domain findings; affected domains were psychological, social, environment and independence. Self-stigma appears as "self-deprecating labels." — [DOI 10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1783271](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1783271)
- Wheaton, Holland. "Women's and girls' ADHD diagnosis journeys: a mother-daughter autoethnography." *Medical Humanities* 2026. Frames repeated misdiagnosis as **epistemic injustice**: women's experiential knowledge was "frequently sidelined." — [DOI 10.1136/medhum-2025-013423](https://doi.org/10.1136/medhum-2025-013423)

**Menopause and hormones in ADHD** [PR] (see also Q5)
- Kini-Seery, Trevaskis, Kilbride, Wrigley et al. "'Hormones rule me': A qualitative exploration of the impact of perimenopause on women with ADHD." *Women's Health* 2026;22. 19 cisgender Irish women with ADHD (age 29–62, mean 47.2), reflexive thematic analysis. Four themes, including the impact on ADHD and mental health, bodily and sexual symptoms, and the need for integrated care. One theme suggested perimenopause "**could bring new compassion and understanding for ADHD**." — [DOI 10.1177/17455057261450178](https://doi.org/10.1177/17455057261450178)
- Osianlis E, Thomas …, Bellgrove MA et al. "ADHD in females: Survey findings on symptoms across hormonal life stages." *J Psychiatr Res* 2025;193:208–215. N=600 females with ADHD diagnosis. **97.5%** perceived worsening ADHD symptoms in menopause and 70.4% postpartum. Cross-sectional ASRS scores were similar across phases, so perceived change was not matched by cross-sectional severity differences. — [DOI 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2025.11.035](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2025.11.035)
- Dorani F, Bijlenga D, Beekman ATF, van Someren EJW, Kooij JJS. "Prevalence of hormone-related mood disorder symptoms in women with ADHD." *J Psychiatr Res* 2021;133:10–15. 209 women with ADHD (18–71). High PMDD and PPD prevalence, and more severe climacteric symptoms (anxiety, depression, sexual dysfunction, vasomotor, somatic). — [DOI 10.1016/j.jpsychires.2020.12.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2020.12.005)
- **Contradictory evidence**: Chapman, Gupta, Hunter, Dommett. "Examining the Link Between ADHD Symptoms and Menopausal Experiences." *J Atten Disord* 2025;29(14):1263–1277. 656 women aged 45–60 (245 with ADHD diagnosis). After FDR correction there were **no significant effects** of ADHD diagnosis on menopausal complaints at any stage, though ADHD symptoms correlated with menopausal complaints across all participants. — [DOI 10.1177/10870547251355006](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547251355006); contrasts with Boyd, Wrigley, Kilbride, Mulligan et al., *Arch Womens Ment Health* 2026, N=602 (377 self-reported ADHD): **greater menopausal symptom severity** in ADHD (t=9.61, p<.001). — [DOI 10.1007/s00737-026-01718-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-026-01718-x)
- Reviews and clinical guidance [PR-review]: Kooij JJS et al. (Eunethydis Female ADHD SIG), *Front Glob Womens Health* 2025;6:1613628. Hormonal transitions exacerbate ADHD symptoms and mood, and estrogen–dopamine interplay may explain lower-estrogen cognitive effects. — [DOI 10.3389/fgwh.2025.1613628](https://doi.org/10.3389/fgwh.2025.1613628); Wynchank D, de Jong M, Kooij JJS, *Eur Psychiatry* 2025;69(1):e1, practical assessment tools for hormonal fluctuations. — [DOI 10.1192/j.eurpsy.2025.10120](https://doi.org/10.1192/j.eurpsy.2025.10120); Krebs, Donnellan-Fernandez, integrative review across women's lifespan, *BMC Womens Health* 2025;25:593. — [DOI 10.1186/s12905-025-04123-1](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-025-04123-1)

### Inferences
- Young's stage model is the clearest empirical basis for "grief for what might have been" in late ADHD diagnosis. It is based on only 8 clinically referred UK adults, and later women-focused studies emphasize validation and self-compassion more than staged grief.
- In midlife ADHD, the shift from "flawed character" to "hormonally sensitive neurotype" is a key reframing. The empirical picture on whether ADHD worsens menopause is mixed: self-report surveys say yes, the controlled cross-sectional comparison by Chapman et al. says not at group level.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study centred on "what might have been" or counterfactual grief as its main construct in late ADHD. It appears as a stage (Young) or theme rather than a measured outcome.
- Popular clinicians named in the brief (Sari Solden, *Women with Attention Deficit Disorder*; Ellen Littman, co-author of *Understanding Girls with ADHD*): my searches returned nothing usable, so I cannot cite specific claims from them here.
- Studies of people diagnosed with ADHD over 60 are almost absent.

## Q3. Is there research or credible writing on late AuDHD diagnosis specifically?

### Takeaway
Yes, but it is very small. Emma Craddock's two IPA papers (six late-diagnosed women, email interviews) are explicitly the first on AuDHD lived experience. They describe a **fragmented or divided identity** ("two separate parts of my brain"), ambivalence from occupying a "residual" diagnostic category, (peri)menopause as a catalyst for diagnosis, and diagnosis as overcoming **epistemic injustice**. A few mixed-sample studies include small dual-diagnosis subgroups, and a 2026 systematic review spans autism and/or ADHD.

### Cited Findings
- Craddock E. "Being a Woman Is 100% Significant to My Experiences of Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder and Autism: Exploring the Gendered Implications of an Adulthood Combined Autism and ADHD Diagnosis." *Qualitative Health Research* 2024;34(14):1442–1455. IPA of email interviews with **six late-diagnosed AuDHD women**. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/10497323241253412](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412)
  - Being undiagnosed AuDHD was "a **confusing and traumatising** experience with profound and enduring repercussions." Findings cover the "inextricability of femininity and neurotypicality" and the gendered burden of masking, as well as the harms of *insufficient* masking. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412)
  - "The impact of female hormones exacerbated participants' struggles with **(peri)menopause often being a catalyst for seeking diagnosis after decades of trauma**." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412)
  - "The **epistemic injustice** of not knowing they were neurodivergent compounded this trauma. Diagnosis enabled participants to overcome epistemic injustice and moved them into a **feminist standpoint**." The paper calls for trauma-informed pre- and post-diagnosis support. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412)
- Craddock E. "Navigating residual diagnostic categories: The lived experiences of women diagnosed with autism and ADHD in adulthood." *Health* (London) 2026;30(2):235–257 (online April 2025). Same six-woman sample, IPA. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/13634593251336163](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634593251336163)
  - AuDHD is conceptualized as a **residual category** not formally represented in diagnostic systems. This "liminal diagnostic space" produces ambivalence and "fragmented identities shaped more by societal narratives than by interpersonal perceptions." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634593251336163)
  - Themes and quotes: contradictions ("**two separate parts of my brain**"), complementarity ("**two sides of the same coin**"), and tension between neurodiversity and medicalization ("**autism is a part of me, ADHD is an add-on**"). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634593251336163)
  - Resolution strategies: seeking "objective understanding" and distinguishing neurodivergent from neurotypical identities. The paper reports a "fluid and dynamic understanding of AuDHD" and claims to be "the first study to explore the lived experiences of adults diagnosed with both autism and ADHD." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/13634593251336163)
- French B, Cassidy S. "'Going Through Life on Hard Mode'—The Experience of Late Diagnosis of Autism and/or ADHD: A Qualitative Study." *Autism in Adulthood* 2026;8(1):127–136 (online 2024). 13 late-diagnosed adults (5 autistic, 5 ADHD, **3 dual**) plus 7 health professionals; reflexive thematic analysis. [PR] — [DOI 10.1089/aut.2024.0085](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2024.0085)
  - Five themes: relationships and mental well-being; lifelong mental-health impact; "**understanding—the answer to postdiagnosis changes**"; "the flip side of diagnosis—the **burden of a label**"; and "**are you ready—the importance of timing**." The authors cite >2.5 million UK adults with undiagnosed neurodevelopmental conditions. Benefits include "finding a community." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2024.0085)
- Meldrum P, Johnson BP, Lo BCY, Bedelis ML, Rabba AS. "'You Become Yourself, Your Full Self, the True Self': A Systematic Review of Neurodivergent Adults' Experiences of Identity Reconstruction Following Diagnosis of Autism and/or ADHD in Adulthood." *Autism in Adulthood* (Sage) 2026. DOI 10.1177/25739581261427260. [PR] Full text and abstract not accessible here. Details come from search-result snippets only: 25 included sources (PRISMA/JBI). One snippet says 12 autism/13 ADHD and another says 12 ADHD/13 autism, so the split is **unverified**. Themes include "reconfiguring the self" and "finding the self through others." Diagnosis tended to move people toward a "more integrated identity," with both shared and condition-specific impacts. Late-diagnosed adults often experienced confusion and doubt during reconstruction. The evidence base underrepresents ethnic minorities. — [Sage abstract page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/25739581261427260); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/402144062); [Synapse Social summary](https://www.synapsesocial.com/papers/69b79e488166e15b153ab597)
- A UK qualitative study of adult females with late diagnosis of ASD and ADHD exists in PMC (PMC12840745). I could not retrieve its details (network block), so I report only its existence. — [PMC12840745](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12840745/)
- [POP] Substack and blog writing on "late diagnosis of AuDHD" turned up in searches (e.g., [Carmen, "Authentically ADHD"](https://carmenauthenticallyadhd.substack.com/p/late-diagnosis-of-audhd-in-adults)). It is not research and is listed only as evidence of community discourse.

### Inferences
- AuDHD research adds a distinctive identity problem to single-diagnosis late diagnosis: **internal plurality or contradiction** (the autistic need for sameness versus the ADHD need for novelty), so integration becomes an explicit identity task. The Meldrum review's title ("your full self, the true self") and its finding of a "more integrated identity" make the same point.
- Craddock's finding that (peri)menopause triggers diagnosis "after decades of trauma" is the most direct peer-reviewed link between midlife, hormones and late AuDHD diagnosis.

### Gaps
- All AuDHD-specific lived-experience data come from N=6 women (one sample across two papers) plus small dual-diagnosis subgroups. There are no AuDHD studies of men or gender-diverse people, and none with age-stratified midlife samples.
- I could not verify the Meldrum review's exact counts or third theme.

## Q4. Do studies describe diagnosis in quasi-spiritual terms (conversion, revelation, rebirth, homecoming, "my people")? How do people talk about purpose, self-compassion and acceptance afterward?

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed study I found frames late diagnosis *explicitly* as spiritual or religious experience. Participant language and article titles nonetheless carry strong transformation, authenticity and absolution metaphors: "the single most important thing that has happened to me in my life," "a way to be me," "you become yourself… the true self," "the not guilty verdict," "re-living life through a new lens." "Homecoming / finding my people" appears as community-belonging themes, not as spiritual framing. Explicitly spiritual meanings of autism appear in Indigenous research (not late-diagnosis-specific) and in popular writing.

### Cited Findings
- **Transformation and centrality language in titles (participant quotes used as titles)** [PR]:
  - "The Single Most Important Thing That Has Happened to Me in My Life" (Arnold et al. 2020) — [DOI 10.1089/aut.2019.0059](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2019.0059)
  - "You Become Yourself, Your Full Self, the True Self" (Meldrum et al. 2026) — [Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/25739581261427260)
  - "A way to be me" (Lilley et al. 2022) — [DOI 10.1177/13623613211050694](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613211050694)
  - "The not guilty verdict" (Punshon et al. 2009), framing diagnosis as exoneration — [DOI 10.1177/1362361309103795](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361309103795)
  - "Being Autistic is Kind of Who You Are, It's an Identity Rather than a Disorder" (Bransgrove & Karakas 2025) — [DOI 10.1007/s10803-025-06865-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-025-06865-1)
- **Before/after "alien" to "self" narratives**: older adults "felt isolated and alien" before diagnosis; afterwards came "reconfiguration of self" (Stagg & Belcher 2019). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/21642850.2019.1684920)
- **Self-compassion and agency**: "transition from being self-critical to self-compassionate, coupled with an increased sense of agency" (Leedham et al., women 40+). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319853442); "validation/self-compassion" as a main benefit for women diagnosed with ADHD (Babinski & Libsack). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547241297897); "self-acceptance after diagnosis" (Attoe & Climie review). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547231161533)
- **Acceptance as a central "quest"**: Harmens et al. 2022 frame autistic women's identification and diagnosis around acceptance by self, others and the autism community. — [DOI 10.1089/aut.2021.0016](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2021.0016)
- **Belonging / "my people"**: Lilley et al.'s companion "Peas in a pod" paper on shared autistic identity and community. — [PMC9986211](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9986211/); "finding a community" as a diagnosis benefit (French & Cassidy). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2024.0085); peer support as a stage-enabler (Bransgrove & Karakas). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-025-06865-1)
- **Purpose and meaning in autistic adults' lives** (not diagnosis-specific): skilled autistic workers (N=45) defined success as growth, community, feeling valued and accepted, "and like their **work had meaning**" (Raymaker et al., *Autism* 2023;27(1):65–75). — [DOI 10.1177/13623613221080813](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613221080813)
- **Explicitly spiritual framing (Indigenous context, not late diagnosis)**: Kitchens JC, Wake D. "Understanding Autism Through Indigenous Relational Ecologies: A Phenomenographic Exploratory Study With Potawatomi Participants." *Autism* 2026;30(10):2532–2544. Five participants (autistic adults, parents, a Knowledge Keeper). One category of description was "**spiritual gift with purpose**," alongside "relational identity and belonging" and "intergenerational family patterning." The authors argue dominant Western frameworks "may miss relational, cultural, and spiritual meanings." [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/13623613261470856](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613261470856)
- **Existential framework for ADHD (children/parents, religious community)**: Dayan H et al. "Neurodiversity-affirming SEA for ADHD…" *Eur Child Adolesc Psychiatry* 2026;35(9):2813–2829. The salutogenic existential approach (SEA) conceptualizes ADHD as "a tension between a tendency toward **freedom** and reality demands (**necessity**)." It aims to enhance sense of coherence and support neurodivergent identity. N=427, Ultra-Orthodox Jewish community in Israel; improvements in child functioning and parental self-efficacy. [PR] Not an adult late-diagnosis study, but it is the clearest peer-reviewed *existential* model of ADHD found. — [DOI 10.1007/s00787-026-03063-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-026-03063-x)
- Most "spirituality + autism" literature in PubMed concerns **parents/caregivers** (e.g., Iranian mothers framing autism as divine purpose or test of faith, reporting "existential meaning and personal growth": Moghaddam-Tabrizi et al., *BMC Psychol* 2026). — [DOI 10.1186/s40359-026-05157-2](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05157-2). It does not cover late-diagnosed adults themselves.
- [POP] Devon Price, *Unmasking Autism: The Power of Embracing Our Hidden Neurodiversity* (Harmony/Octopus, 2022). Price is a social psychologist and autistic author. Publisher descriptions frame unmasking as both personal liberation and social-justice work, aimed at high-masking adults whose camouflage cracks in their 30s–50s. (Publisher and retailer descriptions only; book text not reviewed here.) — [Hachette UK](https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/devon-price/unmasking-autism/9781800960572)

### Inferences
- The *structure* of late-diagnosis narratives closely parallels conversion and rebirth narratives: a pre-diagnosis "false self" or "alien" existence, a revelatory moment that re-reads the entire past, an authentic "true self," and a new community of belonging. The research literature, however, uses secular psychological vocabulary (identity, acceptance, self-compassion, epistemic justice), not spiritual vocabulary. Any spiritual reading is an interpretive overlay, not a reported finding.
- The authenticity language ("true self," "a way to be me") aligns with humanistic and existential themes such as authenticity versus bad faith, which offers a bridge for an existential or spiritual analysis.

### Gaps
- **I found no peer-reviewed study of religion, spirituality or spiritual practice among late-diagnosed autistic, ADHD or AuDHD adults specifically.** This is a clear gap and is worth stating plainly in the report.
- Terms such as "conversion," "rebirth," "revelation" or "homecoming" did not surface in any abstract I read. Full texts may contain such participant quotes, but I could not verify them.

## Q5. Midlife / perimenopause / menopause as a trigger for diagnosis and its link to existential questioning

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed qualitative studies consistently describe menopause as a point where masking and coping collapse ("when my autism broke") and where lifetime adversity "converges at midlife" ("a perfect storm"). (Peri)menopause is explicitly named as a catalyst for seeking AuDHD diagnosis. Existential questioning is implied ("long journey to recognizing autism," life experience converging) but rarely named as such.

### Cited Findings
- Moseley RL, Druce T, Turner-Cobb JM. "'When my autism broke': A qualitative study spotlighting autistic voices on menopause." *Autism* 2020;24(6):1423–1437. Online focus group, 7 autistic women. Autism-related difficulties (sensory sensitivity, socializing, communicating needs) worsened "so dramatically that some participants suggested they found it **impossible to continue to mask**." Also extreme meltdowns, anxiety, depression, suicidality. Described as the first study on menopause in autism. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/1362361319901184](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319901184)
- Moseley RL, Druce T, Turner-Cobb JM. "Autism research is 'all about the blokes and the kids': Autistic women breaking the silence on menopause." *Br J Health Psychol* 2021;26(3):709–726. 17 autistic participants (16 cisgender women), IPA-guided thematic analysis. Theme 1 covers "the **long journey** of our participants to recognizing autism in adulthood." Some had "marked deterioration in daily function and coping skills, mental health, and social engagement," with low menopause awareness and low confidence in professionals. [PR] — [DOI 10.1111/bjhp.12477](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12477)
- Brady MJ, Jenkins CA, Gamble-Turner JM, Moseley RL et al. "'A perfect storm': Autistic experiences of menopause and midlife." *Autism* 2024;28(6):1405–1418. 24 autistic participants (Canada 13, UK 11), co-produced with Autistic Community Research Associates. Themes: (1) complexity, multiplicity and intensity of symptoms; (2) "**Life experience and adversity converging at midlife**"; (3) "the importance of **knowledge and connection**"; (4) barriers to support. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/13623613241244548](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613241244548)
- Moseley RL, Gamble-Turner JM, Kim E, Spencer C. "Autism, ADHD and the menopause." *Post Reproductive Health* 2026;32(3):145–155. Clinical guidance review: the menopausal transition "can amplify aspects of neurodivergence," worsens co-occurring conditions, and is a moment to "recognise undiagnosed individuals." [PR-review] — [DOI 10.1080/20533691.2026.2631222](https://doi.org/10.1080/20533691.2026.2631222)
- Craddock 2024: "(peri)menopause often being a **catalyst for seeking diagnosis** after decades of trauma" (AuDHD women). [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/10497323241253412](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412)
- ADHD perimenopause: "Hormones rule me" (Kini-Seery et al. 2026). One theme reports perimenopause could bring "new compassion and understanding for ADHD." [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/17455057261450178](https://doi.org/10.1177/17455057261450178); Kelly et al. 2026 (women 45–58): hormonal fluctuations affected symptom severity and medication efficacy. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/10870547261469625](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547261469625)
- Quantitative ADHD–menopause evidence is conflicting (Osianlis 2025: 97.5% perceived worsening; Boyd 2026: higher severity; Chapman 2025: no group difference). See Q2. — [Osianlis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2025.11.035); [Boyd](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00737-026-01718-x); [Chapman](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547251355006)

### Inferences
- Midlife works as a double turning point. Biologically, hormonal change erodes compensatory capacity and masking. Biographically, accumulated adversity and the developmental task of midlife life review converge. The "perfect storm" framing fits well with existential "midlife crisis" and life-review frameworks, but the papers do not use those frameworks explicitly.
- Kini-Seery's "new compassion" theme suggests that menopause can be a site of meaning-making and self-compassion, not only deterioration.

### Gaps
- No study I found asks directly about existential or spiritual questioning during neurodivergent menopause.
- Trans, nonbinary and intersex experiences of menopause in neurodivergent people are barely represented (Moseley 2021 had 16 of 17 participants cisgender women; Kini-Seery limited to cisgender women).

## Q6. Masking, burnout and unmasking as identity work; links to spiritual practice or meaning

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed work defines autistic burnout as chronic exhaustion, loss of skills and reduced stimulus tolerance caused by cumulative load. Recovery is associated with acceptance, reduced expectations and "doing things in an autistic way/unmasking." Gendered masking is central to late diagnosis in women and gender-diverse people. Unmasking appears in the research as identity and recovery work. Links to spiritual practice are not documented in peer-reviewed studies I found.

### Cited Findings
- Raymaker DM, Teo AR, Steckler NA, Lentz B et al. "'Having All of Your Internal Resources Exhausted Beyond Measure and Being Left with No Clean-Up Crew': Defining Autistic Burnout." *Autism in Adulthood* 2020;2(2):132–143. CBPR thematic analysis of 19 interviews and 19 public internet sources. Primary characteristics: "**chronic exhaustion, loss of skills, and reduced tolerance to stimulus**," caused by life stressors adding to cumulative load plus barriers to relief. Impacts include suicidal behavior. Recovery was associated with "acceptance and social support, time off/reduced expectations, and **doing things in an autistic way/unmasking**." Distinct from occupational burnout or depression. [PR] — [DOI 10.1089/aut.2019.0079](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2019.0079)
- Leedham et al.: "pretending to be normal and fitting in" as the core pre-diagnosis experience; the title quote is "I was **exhausted** trying to figure it out." [PR] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319853442)
- Harmens et al. 2022: "the **exhaustion** resulting from trying to be accepted and be understood." [PR] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2021.0016)
- Craddock 2024: the "gendered burden, discomfort, and adverse consequences of masking, along with the adverse outcomes of insufficient masking," and the "inextricability of femininity and neurotypicality." [PR] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412)
- Tien et al. 2025: female socialization "directly led to the suppression of their autistic traits" (genderqueer-majority sample). [PR] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1089/aut.2024.0178)
- Moseley et al. 2020: menopause made it "impossible to continue to mask" (involuntary unmasking). [PR] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1362361319901184)
- Autistic burnout is now a consensus "high-priority outcome" for evaluating services for autistic adults (53-expert CBPR-nested Delphi; Nicolaidis et al., *Autism* 2025;29(8):1959–1972). [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/13623613251322082](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613251322082)
- GPs with lived experience of burnout (N=16) named "neurodivergence specifically likely autism," perfectionism, trauma and imposter syndrome as predisposing factors. The paper's keywords include "**spiritual health**" (Whitehead & Hanratty, *Family Practice* 2026). [PR] — [DOI 10.1093/fampra/cmag012](https://doi.org/10.1093/fampra/cmag012)
- [POP] Price's *Unmasking Autism* frames unmasking as a values-driven, liberatory identity project (publisher description). — [Hachette UK](https://www.hachette.co.uk/titles/devon-price/unmasking-autism/9781800960572)

### Inferences
- Unmasking reads as identity work aimed at authenticity, and it is also a recovery mechanism for burnout (Raymaker). This gives an empirically grounded link between diagnosis, unmasking, and the "true self" theme in Q4.
- Burnout often precedes or prompts diagnosis in midlife (via menopause or accumulated load), so the turning point is frequently reached through crisis. That pattern matches "breakdown-to-breakthrough" narratives in post-traumatic growth (PTG) and spiritual-emergency literatures. This is an inference, not a reported finding.

### Gaps
- I found no empirical studies linking unmasking to contemplative or spiritual practice (e.g., meditation, religious community, ritual) in late-diagnosed adults.

## Q7. Post-traumatic growth, meaning-making or existential psychology frameworks applied to neurodivergent late diagnosis

### Takeaway
Formal PTG, meaning-making (e.g., Park) or existential-psychology frameworks have barely been applied empirically to late diagnosis. The closest peer-reviewed anchors are: Young's stage-of-acceptance model (ADHD); the Cognitive Adaptation Model (Taylor 1983), against which Bransgrove & Karakas tested a new autism identity framework; epistemic-injustice and feminist-standpoint theory (Craddock); trauma framings (Lilley; Craddock); and a salutogenic-existential model of ADHD (Dayan, but in children and parents). PTG after late diagnosis appears so far only in popular or professional commentary.

### Cited Findings
- Young et al. 2007: six-stage acceptance model (relief → turmoil → anger → grief → anxiety → acceptance), a grief or adjustment framework. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/1087054707305172](https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054707305172)
- Bransgrove & Karakas 2025: a new post-diagnosis identity-stage framework compared against Taylor's **Cognitive Adaptation Model** (which centres on search for meaning, mastery and self-enhancement after threatening events). [PR] — [DOI 10.1007/s10803-025-06865-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10803-025-06865-1)
- Craddock 2024: **epistemic injustice** (not knowing one's own neurotype) compounded trauma. Diagnosis moved women into a **feminist standpoint**, a political form of meaning-making and purpose. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/10497323241253412](https://doi.org/10.1177/10497323241253412)
- Lilley et al. 2022: trauma shaped self-concept, and diagnosis enabled positive re-narration. [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/13623613211050694](https://doi.org/10.1177/13623613211050694)
- Dayan et al. 2026: salutogenic existential approach (SEA) to ADHD, using freedom versus necessity, sense of coherence and identity affirmation. [PR] — [DOI 10.1007/s00787-026-03063-x](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-026-03063-x)
- Dangmann, Skogli, Holthe, Steffenak, "Life Gets Better: Important Resilience Factors When Growing Up With ADHD," *J Atten Disord* 2024;28(8):1198–1209 (emerging adults 21–24, not late-diagnosed). Resilience factors include acceptance, seeing positive attributes, and "participating in **meaningful activities**." [PR] — [DOI 10.1177/10870547241246645](https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547241246645)
- [POP] *Psychology Today* ("The Neurodivergent Therapist," June 2024), "Post-Traumatic Growth After a Late Autism Diagnosis." Search summary: the piece argues late ASD/ADHD diagnosis can prompt PTG when the experience is framed around purpose rather than deficiency. Not empirical. — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-neurodivergent-therapist/202406/post-traumatic-growth-after-a-late-autism-diagnosis)
- [POP/professional] British Psychological Society, *The Psychologist*, "You have a diagnosis… now what?" Search summary: describes a **life-review** process in which past difficulties are reattributed to autism and ableism rather than personal failure, and invokes PTG research. Not an empirical study. — [BPS](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/you-have-diagnosis-now-what)
- [POP] *Psychology Today* ("Living Neurodivergence," July 2023), "Making Sense of the Past as a Late-Diagnosed Autistic Adult." — [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/living-neurodivergence/202307/making-sense-of-the-past-as-a-late-diagnosed-autistic-adult)
- General PTG background (not neurodivergence-specific): spirituality was associated with deliberate rumination and PTG, but not with intrusive rumination or PTSD symptoms (PLOS ONE 2022, N=96 students). [PR] — [PMC9352075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9352075/)

### Inferences
- The empirical findings fit Tedeschi & Calhoun's PTG domains closely: new self-understanding (personal strength), "finding my people" (relating to others), advocacy or a feminist standpoint (new possibilities), and self-compassion and acceptance (appreciation of life). Spiritual or existential change, the fifth PTG domain, is the one domain the literature has not explored. A report could present this as a hypothesis-generating mapping, clearly labelled as inference.
- The PTG–spirituality link via *deliberate* rumination is relevant because late diagnosis invites deliberate autobiographical re-reading ("re-living life through a new lens"). This is a theoretical bridge, not an established finding in neurodivergent samples.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed study found that measures PTG (e.g., PTGI) or uses a meaning-making model (e.g., Park's) in late-diagnosed autistic, ADHD or AuDHD adults.**
- No studies found applying Yalom, Frankl, Erikson (integrity versus despair) or Butler's life review to late neurodivergent diagnosis.
- The popular PTG and BPS pieces could not be fetched in full (web-search summaries only), so their authors and exact claims should be checked before quoting.
